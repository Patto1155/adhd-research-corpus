# Executive function and ADHD: why can't I plan or get started?

> **Short answer:** On average, people with ADHD score lower on executive function tests (holding information in mind, stopping an action, switching tasks, planning) by a medium amount. Some score normally yet still struggle in daily life, partly because lab tests and everyday ratings measure different things. Executive difficulties are a common part of ADHD, not the whole of it.

## What the evidence shows

**Medium average differences on executive tests.** A meta-analysis of 83 studies (about 3,700 people with ADHD and 3,000 without, mostly children) found weaker average performance across executive tasks, with effect sizes of *d* = 0.46 to 0.69 [1]. By this page's own calculation (not a figure from the paper), the two groups' scores still overlap by roughly 73–82%. The clearest differences were in:

- **response inhibition**: stopping an action that has already started;
- **vigilance**: keeping attention on a dull task;
- **working memory**: holding and using information over a few seconds, like a phone number while you dial it;
- **planning**: organising steps towards a goal.

These differences held in clinic and community samples, and were not explained by IQ, school achievement or other conditions [1].

**Adults show similar-sized differences, not only on executive tasks.** A meta-analysis of 13 studies in adults found medium effects for verbal fluency (*d* = 0.62), inhibition (*d* = 0.64 and 0.89) and set shifting (*d* = 0.65) [2]. Adults with ADHD also differed by similar amounts on *non*-executive measures, such as consistency of responding (*d* = 0.57) and speed of naming colours (*d* = 0.62). The authors concluded that adult difficulties are not confined to executive function [2].

**Weaknesses are "neither necessary nor sufficient".** The effects are medium rather than large, and not everyone with ADHD performs poorly on these tests; a checked figure for how many do was not found. So the authors of the large meta-analysis concluded that executive weaknesses do not cause all ADHD [1]. More is on [why no two ADHD brains are alike](../02-whats-happening-in-my-brain/why-no-two-adhd-brains-are-alike.md).

**Tests and daily life barely agree.** A review of 20 studies compared lab tests of executive function with questionnaires about executive problems in everyday life [3]. Only 24% of 286 correlations were statistically significant, and the median correlation was 0.19, which is weak [3].

**The two measure different things.** The authors concluded that tests capture how efficiently you process information in a quiet, structured room with an examiner prompting you, while ratings capture how you pursue goals in messy real settings [3]. This is one reason people with ADHD can "test fine" yet struggle with bills, deadlines and housework.

**Genetic risk for ADHD is linked to executive performance.** In the largest genetic study, common-variant risk for ADHD was associated with weaker complex thinking, such as verbal reasoning, and with a range of executive functions including attention [4]. This links the genetics to the thinking differences, at population level.

## What is uncertain or debated

- **Which comes first.** It is unclear whether executive difficulties cause the other symptoms, are one route among several, or are a shared consequence of something more basic, such as variable attention or motivation.
- **Motivation and effort matter.** Test performance in ADHD can shift with reward and interest, which complicates the idea of a fixed "deficit". See [Time and waiting in ADHD: why do I lose track and hate waiting?](time-waiting-and-rewards.md)
- **Specificity.** Executive weaknesses are also found in many other conditions, including depression, anxiety and autism, so they do not identify ADHD.
- **Children's data applied to adults.** Most studies are in children. The adult meta-analysis is older and smaller (13 studies) [2].
- **"Executive dysfunction" in popular use** often describes a feeling of being unable to start a task. That experience is common in ADHD, but it is not what the lab tests above measure, and it has not been studied as a defined construct.

## What this means for you

If you struggle to start, organise and finish, that is consistent with what ADHD research finds on average, and it is not a character flaw or a lack of effort. If a neuropsychological test came back normal, that does not mean your difficulties are imagined; the tests and real life measure different things. It also means test scores alone cannot confirm or exclude ADHD.
