# Executive function and ADHD: why can't I plan or get started?

> **Short answer:** On average, people with ADHD score lower on executive function tests (holding information in mind, stopping an action, switching tasks, planning) by a small-to-medium amount. Many score in the normal range yet still struggle in daily life, partly because lab tests and everyday ratings measure different things. Executive difficulties are a common part of ADHD, not the whole of it, and they are not unique to it.

## What the evidence shows

**Medium average differences on executive tests.** A meta-analysis of 83 studies (3,734 people with ADHD and 2,969 without, mostly children) found weaker average performance on every executive task, with effect sizes of *d* = 0.46 to 0.69 [1]. An effect size (*d*) is the gap between the group averages measured in standard deviations; by this page's own calculation (not a figure from the paper), the two groups' scores still overlap by roughly 73–82%. The clearest differences were in:

- **response inhibition**: stopping an action that has already started;
- **vigilance**: keeping attention on a dull task;
- **working memory**: holding and using information over a few seconds, like a phone number while you dial it;
- **planning**: organising steps towards a goal.

**These differences are not explained by IQ.** They held in clinic and community samples, and were not explained by IQ, school achievement or other conditions [1].

**A review of 34 meta-analyses found the same pattern.** Pooling 253 comparisons from 34 meta-analyses, 96% favoured the group without ADHD, with an average effect size of 0.45 [2]. The largest average gaps were in reaction-time variability, working memory, response inhibition, vigilance, and planning and organisation, at about 0.5 to 0.66 [2]. Meta-analyses funded by drug companies reported larger effects than others, which is a reason to read the higher figures with some caution [2].

**Working memory differences are among the larger ones in children.** A meta-analysis of 26 studies in children and teenagers found larger gaps for holding and manipulating visual and spatial information (effect sizes 0.85 and 1.06) than for words and numbers (0.47 and 0.43) [3]. These differences were not explained by language learning problems or lower IQ [3].

**Adults show similar-sized differences, not only on executive tasks.** A meta-analysis of 13 studies in adults found medium effects for verbal fluency (*d* = 0.62), inhibition (*d* = 0.64 and 0.89) and set shifting, meaning switching between tasks or rules (*d* = 0.65) [4]. Adults with ADHD also differed by similar amounts on *non*-executive measures, such as consistency of responding (*d* = 0.57) and speed of naming colours (*d* = 0.62), so adult difficulties are not confined to executive function [4]. A later meta-analysis of 38 adult studies found moderate working memory differences, and concluded that these persist into adulthood [5].

**Weaknesses are "neither necessary nor sufficient".** The effects are medium rather than large, and not everyone with ADHD performs poorly on these tests, so the authors of the large meta-analysis concluded that executive weaknesses do not cause all ADHD [1]. A review by some of the same researchers argued that, because the two groups' scores overlap so much, only a subgroup of people with ADHD may have a true executive deficit [6]. More is on [why no two ADHD brains are alike](../02-whats-happening-in-my-brain/why-no-two-adhd-brains-are-alike.md).

**How many people with ADHD have an executive weakness is disputed.** Older estimates, summarised in a later study, put it at about a third to a half of children with ADHD [7]. That study of 136 children, which used several tests per skill and allowed for test unreliability, found 89% of children with ADHD were impaired on at least one of working memory (62%), inhibition (27%) or task-switching (38%) [7]. The second figure comes from one small sample, so the true share is uncertain and depends heavily on how "impaired" is defined.

**Tests and daily life barely agree.** A review of 20 studies compared lab tests of executive function with questionnaires about executive problems in everyday life [8]. Only 68 (24%) of 286 correlations were statistically significant, and the median correlation was 0.19, which is weak [8].

**The two measure different things.** The authors concluded that tests capture how efficiently you process information in a quiet, structured room with an examiner prompting you, while ratings capture how successfully you pursue goals in messy real settings [8]. This is one reason people with ADHD can "test fine" yet struggle with bills, deadlines and housework.

**Genetic risk for ADHD is linked to executive performance.** In a genome-wide study of 38,691 people with ADHD and 186,843 without, common-variant risk for ADHD was associated with weaker complex thinking, such as verbal reasoning, and with a range of executive functions including attention [9]. This links the genetics to the thinking differences, at population level. Heritability itself is covered on [genes and inheritance](../02-whats-happening-in-my-brain/genes-and-inheritance.md).

## What is uncertain or debated

- **Which comes first.** It is unclear whether executive difficulties cause the other symptoms, are one route among several, or are a shared consequence of something more basic, such as variable attention or motivation [6].
- **Motivation and effort matter.** A review of 22 studies (1,181 children) found that rewards improved task performance in children with and without ADHD, somewhat more so in ADHD [10]. That complicates the idea of a fixed "deficit". See [Time and waiting in ADHD: why do I lose track and hate waiting?](time-waiting-and-rewards.md)
- **Specificity.** Executive weaknesses are also found in other conditions. A meta-analysis of 113 studies found them reliably in depression (effect sizes 0.32 to 0.97) [11], and a meta-analysis of 180 studies found executive delays across childhood neurodevelopmental conditions, including autism, with ADHD standing out mainly on attention, inhibition, planning and working memory [12]. So these tests do not identify ADHD.
- **Children's data applied to adults.** Most studies are in children. The adult meta-analyses are older and smaller (13 and 38 studies) [4, 5].
- **"Executive dysfunction" in popular use** often describes a feeling of being unable to start a task. That experience is common in ADHD, but it is not what the lab tests above measure, and it has not been studied as a defined construct.

## What this means for you

If you struggle to start, organise and finish, that is consistent with what ADHD research finds on average, and it is not a character flaw or a lack of effort. If a neuropsychological test came back normal, that does not mean your difficulties are imagined; the tests and real life measure different things. It also means test scores alone cannot confirm or exclude ADHD.
