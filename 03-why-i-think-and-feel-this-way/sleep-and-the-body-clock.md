# Sleep and ADHD: why can't I fall asleep, and is sleep the cause?

> **Short answer:** Sleep problems are common in ADHD, in children and adults, especially taking a long time to fall asleep. On average, body clocks run later, with the hormone melatonin rising later in the evening, though total sleep time is often not shorter. Poor sleep can also cause ADHD-like attention problems, and which causes which is not settled.

## What the evidence shows

**Children with ADHD have more sleep problems.** A meta-analysis of 16 studies (722 children with ADHD and 638 without) found children with ADHD had more bedtime resistance, more trouble falling asleep and more night waking, as reported by parents [1]. On objective measures, they took longer to fall asleep on actigraphy (a wrist-worn movement sensor) and had more breathing pauses during sleep (a higher apnoea–hypopnoea index) [1].

**Adults with ADHD report worse sleep on almost every measure.** The first meta-analysis of sleep in adults pooled 13 studies [2]. Adults with ADHD differed on 7 of 9 self-reported sleep measures, including daytime sleepiness. On actigraphy they took longer to fall asleep and slept less efficiently, meaning more time awake in bed, but total sleep time did not differ [2].

**Body clocks run later, on average.** A systematic review of 62 studies (4,462 patients with ADHD) found ADHD was consistently associated with a later chronotype ("night owl" preference) and a later dim-light melatonin onset [3]. Dim-light melatonin onset (DLMO) is the evening time at which melatonin starts to rise; it is the standard marker of the body clock.

**Adults who struggle to fall asleep showed the clearest delay.** In one study of 40 adults with ADHD, 31 reported chronic difficulty falling asleep (sleep-onset insomnia) [4]. Those 31 had a later start and end to their sleep and later melatonin onset than both the other adults with ADHD and healthy controls [4]. How large the delay was is not given in the abstract, and a clinic sample this small cannot say how common the pattern is among adults with ADHD generally.

**Only a small number of studies link body-clock genes to ADHD.** The same systematic review found a handful of genetic association studies linking variants in circadian clock genes with ADHD symptoms [3]. This is early evidence.

**Stimulant medicines can delay sleep.** In children, methylphenidate was associated with a higher risk of sleep problems than placebo [5]. More is on [medication safety](../06-treatment-what-works/medication-safety.md).

## What is uncertain or debated

- **Which way does it run?** A later body clock might contribute to ADHD symptoms, ADHD might push sleep later, or a shared biology might drive both. The research is mostly cross-sectional, which cannot tell these apart.
- **Genetic studies disagree.** Mendelian randomisation uses genetic variants linked to a trait as a natural experiment, to test cause and effect. One such study estimated that a genetic tendency to insomnia raised the odds of ADHD by about a third (odds ratio 1.33), with a weaker hint of ADHD shortening sleep [6]; an earlier one found no clear effect in either direction for ADHD [7]. They used different genetic data and methods, and both describe lifelong tendencies, not the effect of a bad night or of treating sleep.
- **Sleep problems can look like ADHD.** Short or disrupted sleep impairs attention and mood in anyone, and conditions such as sleep apnoea and delayed sleep phase disorder can resemble ADHD. See [is it ADHD or something else](../05-what-often-comes-with-it/is-it-adhd-or-something-else.md).
- **What guidelines say.** A 2021 NICE surveillance review noted that body-clock (circadian rhythm) disorders cause delayed sleep onset and that their symptoms can overlap with those of ADHD [8]. A checked guideline recommendation on screening for sleep problems during ADHD assessment was not found for this page.
- **Treatments for the body clock.** Melatonin may help sleep onset in ADHD, but the systematic review found few trials [3]. Whether shifting the body clock also improves daytime ADHD symptoms has been tested in one small trial, with mixed results: in 51 adults with ADHD and a delayed body clock, three weeks of low-dose melatonin moved the clock earlier and reduced self-rated ADHD symptoms by 14%, but melatonin plus morning bright light moved the clock further without changing symptoms [9]. Two weeks after treatment stopped, both the clock and symptoms were back where they started [9].
- **Adults versus children.** The child evidence is larger. The adult meta-analysis included 13 studies [2].
- **Medication versus ADHD itself.** Many studies include people on stimulants, which can affect sleep, making it hard to isolate ADHD's own effect.

## What this means for you

If you lie awake for a long time and feel most alert late at night, that fits a well-documented pattern in ADHD, at group level. Because poor sleep also causes attention problems, sleep is worth raising in any assessment, both to avoid misdiagnosis and because it may be treatable in its own right. Decisions about melatonin or adjusting medication timing are for a prescriber.
