# Why can't I fall asleep, and could poor sleep be what is wrong with me?

> **Short answer:** Sleep problems are common in ADHD in both children and adults, especially taking a long time to fall asleep. On average, people with ADHD have later body clocks, with the hormone melatonin rising later in the evening. Total sleep time is often not shorter. Poor sleep can also produce ADHD-like problems with attention, so sleep and ADHD are hard to untangle, and which causes which is not settled.

## What the evidence shows

**Children with ADHD have more sleep problems.** A meta-analysis of 16 studies (722 children with ADHD and 638 without) found children with ADHD had more bedtime resistance, more trouble falling asleep and more night waking, as reported by parents [1]. On objective measures, they took longer to fall asleep on actigraphy (a wrist-worn movement sensor) and had more breathing pauses during sleep (a higher apnoea–hypopnoea index) [1]. Later studies of unmedicated children using overnight sleep recordings have been less consistent.

**Adults with ADHD report worse sleep on almost every measure.** The first meta-analysis of sleep in adults pooled 13 studies [2]. Adults with ADHD differed on 7 of 9 self-reported sleep measures, including daytime sleepiness. On actigraphy they took longer to fall asleep and slept less efficiently, meaning more time awake in bed. Total sleep time did not differ [2].

**Body clocks run later, on average.** A systematic review of 62 studies (4,462 patients with ADHD) found ADHD was consistently associated with a later chronotype ("night owl" preference) and a later dim-light melatonin onset [3]. Dim-light melatonin onset (DLMO) is the evening time at which melatonin starts to rise; it is the standard marker of the body clock. In one study of adults with ADHD, those with chronic difficulty falling asleep (sleep-onset insomnia) had a later start and end to their sleep and later melatonin onset than both other adults with ADHD and healthy controls [4]. Figures for how many adults with ADHD have this delay, and by how much, circulate from later citations of the study but were not confirmed in its abstract, so they are not quoted here.

**Only a small number of studies link body-clock genes to ADHD.** The same systematic review found a handful of genetic association studies linking variants in circadian clock genes with ADHD symptoms [3]. This is early evidence.

**Stimulant medicines can delay sleep.** In children, methylphenidate was associated with a higher risk of sleep problems than placebo [5]. See [medication safety](../06-treatment-what-works/medication-safety.md).

## What is uncertain or debated

- **Which way does it run?** A later body clock might contribute to ADHD symptoms, ADHD might push sleep later, or a shared biology might drive both. The research is mostly cross-sectional, which cannot tell these apart.
- **Sleep problems can look like ADHD.** Short or disrupted sleep impairs attention and mood in anyone, and conditions such as sleep apnoea and delayed sleep phase disorder can resemble ADHD. A 2021 NICE surveillance review noted that body-clock (circadian rhythm) disorders cause delayed sleep onset and that their symptoms can overlap with those of ADHD [6]; a checked guideline recommendation on screening for sleep problems during ADHD assessment was not found for this page. See [is it ADHD or something else?](../05-what-often-comes-with-it/is-it-adhd-or-something-else.md).
- **Treatments for the body clock.** Melatonin may help sleep onset in ADHD, but the systematic review found few trials [3]. Whether shifting the body clock also improves daytime ADHD symptoms has hardly been tested.
- **Adults versus children.** The child evidence is larger. The adult meta-analysis included 13 studies [2].
- **Medication versus ADHD itself.** Many studies include people on stimulants, which can affect sleep, making it hard to isolate ADHD's own effect.

## What this means for you

If you lie awake for a long time and feel most alert late at night, that fits a well-documented pattern in ADHD, at group level. Because poor sleep also causes attention problems, sleep is worth raising in any assessment, both to avoid misdiagnosis and because it may be treatable in its own right. Decisions about melatonin or adjusting medication timing are for a prescriber.
