# ADHD, work and study: does treatment or support help?

> **Short answer:** Possibly a little, but it is barely tested. Registry studies find slightly less long-term unemployment during medicated periods; one university trial found a therapy-and-mentoring programme helped students; workplace and exam adjustments are almost untested or show little ADHD-specific benefit. On average, adults with ADHD earn less and are less likely to finish university, though many do well.

## What the evidence shows

**Income and unemployment, on average.** A Swedish registry study followed about 1.2 million people, roughly 28,900 of them with ADHD, for 6 to 16 years after they left compulsory school [1]. On average, those with ADHD had about 17% lower annual income and about 12 more days of unemployment per year [1]. Education explained part of the income gap, but income was still lower than in people with the same level of education [1].

People with ADHD were also much more likely to receive a disability pension (odds ratio 19), mostly explained by co-occurring intellectual disability or other developmental conditions [1]. The abstract does not give the absolute share on disability pension, so the size of that risk in real numbers could not be stated here.

**Fewer degrees, in a US follow-up of children.** The Pittsburgh ADHD Longitudinal Study followed children diagnosed with ADHD into young adulthood [2]. Educational and job attainment was lower than in a comparison group, but varied widely [2]. Figures for degree completion circulate from an advocacy summary of this study, but they could not be checked against the paper, so none are quoted here.

These were children referred to a clinic in the 1980s and 1990s, so the findings may not apply to adults diagnosed later.

**Lower grades and more dropout at university.** A US study followed 201 college students with ADHD and 205 without for four years [3]. Students with ADHD had lower grade averages, a moderate-sized difference (Hedges' *g*, a measure of how far apart two group averages are in units of how spread out people's scores are, of −0.46 and −0.63 in the two comparisons the abstract reports) [3]. Among students with ADHD not taking medication, 49% were still enrolled after eight semesters, against 59% of the comparison group [3].

**Job changes.** Adults with ADHD are often described as changing jobs more often. No peer-reviewed study giving a reliable figure for this was found for this page.

**Medication and work: a small registry signal.** In a Swedish study of 12,875 working-age adults with ADHD, long-term unemployment (90 or more days a year) was about 11% less common during periods when people were taking ADHD medication than when the same people were not (relative risk 0.89) [4]. The abstract gives no absolute rates, so how many people this represents could not be stated.

**The link was not found for everyone or every medicine.** In a separate analysis of medication use over the previous two years, the association was clear in women (0.82) but not in men (0.96, not statistically clear) [4]. A second Swedish study of 221,714 people found lower sickness absence or disability pension only during atomoxetine use (hazard ratio 0.89), not with stimulants [5].

Both Swedish medication-and-work studies compared each person with themselves, which removes fixed differences between people but not changes over time. The home page for this kind of evidence is [what registries show](../06-treatment-what-works/what-registries-show.md).

**Medication and study: a small effect in one registry study.** In Sweden, 930 people with ADHD scored on average 4.8 points higher on the university entrance test (scale 1 to 200) when taking it during a medicated period than during an unmedicated one [6]. An accompanying editorial questioned whether that is practically meaningful [6].

Randomised trials of adult ADHD medication measure symptoms over weeks or months, not careers or degrees; decisions about medication are for you and a prescriber. More is on [how well medication works](../06-treatment-what-works/how-well-medication-works.md).

**Exam adjustments: little evidence of a specific benefit.** A 2021 systematic review of educational accommodations for children and teenagers with ADHD found that most showed no benefit specific to students with ADHD, with reading aloud the exception [7]. An earlier systematic review of students with behavioural difficulties, a wider group than ADHD alone, reported evidence, which its authors call preliminary, that extra time, the most common adjustment, gives students with ADHD no boost beyond what it gives other students and may do more harm than good [8]. Both reviews are of school-age students, so applying them to university is an extrapolation.

**University support: one sizeable trial.** A US trial at two public universities randomly assigned 250 students with ADHD to ACCESS, a two-semester programme combining group cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) with individual mentoring, either straight away or a year later [9]. Those who started at once improved more in ADHD symptoms, executive functioning and use of disability support services, with medium to large effects (Cohen's *d*, a measure like Hedges' *g* above, of 0.39 to 1.21), though depression and anxiety did not clearly change [9]. Outcomes were self-reported by students who knew they were in the programme, against a waiting list, so this supports structured study support rather than any single adjustment; grades and graduation were not among the main outcomes in the abstract.

**Workplace adjustments: almost untested.** A 2022 systematic review of interventions to support adults with ADHD at work found very little research on workplace interventions, and noted that most studies tested medication, which is hard to translate into workplace practice [10].

A 2024 review of 79 studies on employment experiences concluded that people with ADHD can do well with good job fit and support [11]. A charity summary says it excluded intervention trials, which the abstract did not confirm; if so, it cannot show which adjustments work.

## What is uncertain or debated

**Cause.** ADHD travels with other conditions, lower school attainment and social disadvantage. Registry studies adjust for some of these, but cannot fully separate ADHD itself from what comes with it.

**Who is studied.** Most large studies are Swedish or American. Welfare systems, labour markets and university support differ a great deal between countries, and findings may not carry over.

**Whether support helps.** It is plausible that coaching, mentoring, flexible deadlines or quieter workspaces help. Plausible is not the same as tested: no controlled trial of a workplace adjustment for ADHD was found for this page, and the one sizeable university trial tested a whole programme, not a single adjustment [9].

**Your rights.** Whether you must, should or can safely tell an employer or university, and what adjustments the law requires, depends on your country and sometimes your job. That is a legal question, and these pages do not give legal advice.

## What this means for you

The figures describe averages over thousands of people, many of whom are doing well; they do not predict your income or whether you will finish a course.

They do suggest that difficulties at work or study are common, recognised, and partly shaped by things outside you, such as support and job fit. If you are weighing medication partly for work or study, the registry evidence suggests a small average association, not a guarantee, and that is a conversation to have with a prescriber.
