# How does ADHD affect work and study, and does treatment or support change that?

> **Short answer:** On average, across large groups, adults with ADHD earn less, spend more time unemployed and are less likely to finish university than people without ADHD, though many do well and the gaps are averages over very different lives. Registry studies find slightly lower unemployment during periods when people take ADHD medication, but the effect is small and the studies are observational. Evidence on workplace and university adjustments is thin: the most common exam adjustment, extra time, has little evidence of helping students with ADHD specifically. Legal rights and disclosure rules depend on where you live and are outside what this page covers.

## What the evidence shows

**Income and unemployment, on average.** A Swedish registry study followed about 1.2 million people, roughly 28,900 of them with ADHD, for 6 to 16 years after they left compulsory school [1]. On average, those with ADHD had about 17% lower annual income and about 12 more days of unemployment per year [1]. They were also much more likely to receive a disability pension (odds ratio 19), mostly explained by co-occurring intellectual disability or other developmental conditions [1]. The abstract does not give the absolute share on disability pension, so the size of that risk in real numbers could not be stated here. Education explained part of the income gap, but income was still lower than in people with the same level of education [1].

**Fewer degrees, in a US follow-up of children.** The Pittsburgh ADHD Longitudinal Study followed children diagnosed with ADHD into young adulthood [2]. Educational and job attainment was lower than in a comparison group, but varied widely [2]. Figures for degree completion circulate from an advocacy summary of this study, but they could not be checked against the paper, so none are quoted here. These were children referred to a clinic in the 1980s and 1990s, so the findings may not apply to adults diagnosed later.

**Lower grades and more dropout at university.** A US study followed 201 college students with ADHD and 205 without for four years [3]. Students with ADHD had lower grade averages, a moderate-sized difference (Hedges' *g* of −0.46 and −0.63 in the two comparisons the abstract reports) [3]. Among students with ADHD not taking medication, 49% were still enrolled after eight semesters, against 59% of the comparison group [3].

**Job changes.** Adults with ADHD are often described as changing jobs more often. No peer-reviewed study giving a reliable figure for this was found for this page.

**Medication and work: a small registry signal.** In a Swedish study of 12,875 working-age adults with ADHD, long-term unemployment (90 or more days a year) was about 11% less common during periods when people were taking ADHD medication than when the same people were not (relative risk 0.89) [4]. In a separate analysis of medication use over the previous two years, the association was clear in women (0.82) but not in men (0.96, not statistically clear) [4]. The abstract gives no absolute rates, so how many people this represents could not be stated. A second Swedish study of 221,714 people found lower sickness absence or disability pension only during atomoxetine use (hazard ratio 0.89), not with stimulants [5]. Both compared each person with themselves, which removes fixed differences between people but not changes over time. The home page for this kind of evidence is [what registries show](../06-treatment-what-works/what-registries-show.md).

**Medication and study: a small effect in one registry study.** In Sweden, 930 people with ADHD scored on average 4.8 points higher on the university entrance test (scale 1 to 200) when taking it during a medicated period than during an unmedicated one [6]. An accompanying editorial questioned whether that is practically meaningful [6]. Randomised trials of adult ADHD medication measure symptoms over weeks or months, not careers or degrees; see [how well medication works](../06-treatment-what-works/how-well-medication-works.md). Decisions about medication are for you and a prescriber.

**Exam adjustments: little evidence of a specific benefit.** A 2021 systematic review of educational accommodations for children and teenagers with ADHD found that most showed no benefit specific to students with ADHD, with reading aloud the exception [7]. An earlier systematic review concluded that preliminary evidence suggests extra time, the most common adjustment, does not give students with ADHD a boost beyond what it gives other students, and may do more harm than good [10]. These studies are in school-age students and are being applied here to university.

**Workplace adjustments: almost untested.** A 2022 systematic review of interventions to support adults with ADHD at work found very little research on workplace interventions, and noted that most studies tested medication, which is hard to translate into workplace practice [8]. A 2024 review of 79 studies on employment experiences concluded that people with ADHD can do well with good job fit and support, but it excluded intervention trials, so it cannot show which adjustments work [9].

## What is uncertain or debated

**Cause.** ADHD travels with other conditions, lower school attainment and social disadvantage. Registry studies adjust for some of these, but cannot fully separate ADHD itself from what comes with it.

**Who is studied.** Most large studies are Swedish or American. Welfare systems, labour markets and university support differ a great deal between countries, and findings may not carry over.

**Whether support helps.** It is plausible that coaching, mentoring, flexible deadlines or quieter workspaces help. Plausible is not the same as tested, and no controlled trial of a workplace adjustment for ADHD was found for this page.

**Your rights.** Whether you must, should or can safely tell an employer or university, and what adjustments the law requires, depends on your country and sometimes your job. That is a legal question, and this corpus does not give legal advice.

## What this means for you

The figures describe averages over thousands of people, many of whom are doing well; they do not predict your income or whether you will finish a course. They do suggest that difficulties at work or study are common, recognised, and partly shaped by things outside you, such as support and job fit. If you are weighing medication partly for work or study, the registry evidence suggests a modest average association, not a guarantee, and that is a conversation to have with a prescriber.
