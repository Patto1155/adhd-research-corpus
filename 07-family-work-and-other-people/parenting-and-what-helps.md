# Does parent training or school support actually help?

> **Short answer:** Yes, but for different things than many people expect. Parent training reliably improves parenting and reduces children's defiance and behaviour problems, including when judged by people who do not know which families were treated. Its effect on core ADHD symptoms looks moderate when parents rate it, and shrinks to little or nothing when independent, blinded observers do. Classroom behaviour programmes reduce disruptive and off-task behaviour, with smaller effects on grades. Being offered parent training is not a verdict on your parenting.

## What the evidence shows

**Rating bias matters a lot here.** In behavioural treatment studies, the people rating the child (usually parents and teachers) often know who received the treatment and have invested in it. Researchers therefore separate *unblinded* ratings from *probably blinded* ones, made by observers who do not know which group a child was in. A 2013 meta-analysis found that with unblinded ratings, behavioural interventions appeared to reduce ADHD symptoms; with probably-blinded ratings, the effect on core symptoms was not significant [1].

**Parent training improves parenting and conduct, even when blinded.** A meta-analysis of randomised trials of behavioural interventions for children with ADHD found that, on probably-blinded measures, effects held for positive parenting (standardised mean difference, SMD, 0.63), negative parenting (0.43) and child conduct problems (0.31) [2]. An SMD of 0.3 is small: treated and untreated groups overlap by almost 90%. An SMD of 0.6 is moderate, with about 75% overlap. On unblinded ratings, ADHD symptoms improved by SMD 0.35, along with parents' confidence, children's social skills and schoolwork [2]. Parents' own mental wellbeing did not clearly improve [2].

**In preschool children the pattern is the same.** A meta-analysis of 16 studies (1,003 children aged 2.5 to 6) found moderate effects on parent-rated ADHD symptoms (Hedges' g about 0.51), conduct problems (0.40) and negative parenting (0.63), lasting 3 to 12 months [3]. There was no significant effect on independently assessed ADHD symptoms, and the blinded effect on negative parenting was smaller, about 0.33 [3]. <!-- CHECK: the blinded 0.33 figure comes from a later review citing Rimestad, not from Rimestad's abstract --> This evidence is why NICE recommends group parent training as the first-line treatment for children under 5 ([children and medication](children-and-medication.md)).

**Classroom programmes reduce disruptive behaviour.** A meta-analysis restricted to randomised trials (19 trials, 18,094 primary-school children) found small benefits of behavioural classroom programmes on disruptive and on-task behaviour, measured by teacher ratings and classroom observation [4]. These trials studied disruptive behaviour in general, many with whole-class programmes, so they are not specific to ADHD. <!-- CHECK: separate pooled effect sizes for teacher ratings and observations not captured --> A review of 28 studies of school-based approaches for ADHD found the best effects for interventions that combine several elements, such as rewards, feedback and daily report cards shared between school and home [5]. <!-- CHECK: g = 0.37 for multi-component and g = 0.69 for daily report cards were reported in search summaries but could not be confirmed in the abstract; not quoted --> Effects on academic results are generally smaller and less consistent than effects on behaviour.

**Treating a child's ADHD tends to ease parental stress.** Parents of children with ADHD report more stress than other parents, in line with symptom severity ([ADHD in children and teenagers](adhd-in-children-and-teenagers.md)). A 2018 meta-analysis found that interventions for children's ADHD were followed by moderate reductions in parenting stress (d about 0.5) [6]. <!-- CHECK: Theule 2018 figures came from a search snippet; full citation details (authors, volume) not captured -->

## What is uncertain or debated

**Whether blinded measures are fair.** Blinded observers see a child for short periods and may miss changes that parents see daily, so a null blinded result does not prove there is no change at home. Equally, parent ratings may be inflated by hope and effort. Both views appear in the research literature, and the honest summary is that blinded evidence for core-symptom change is weak.

**Which parts of programmes work.** Studies have tried to identify active ingredients, such as praise, consistent consequences and time-out, but no single component has been firmly established.

**Long-term effects.** Most trials follow families for months, not years. Whether parent training changes the long-term course of ADHD is not known.

**Parents who have ADHD themselves.** ADHD is highly heritable, so many parents in these programmes have ADHD traits too. Whether standard programmes work as well for them has been studied little; no review was found for this page.

**Evidence from secondary school.** Most school studies are in primary-age children. Evidence for teenagers is thinner.

## What this means for you

Parent training is offered because it reliably improves the day-to-day relationship and reduces conflict and defiance, which are often what make family life hardest. It is not offered because parenting caused the ADHD; the evidence on causes points elsewhere ([what does and doesn't cause ADHD](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/what-does-and-doesnt-cause-adhd.md)). It is less likely than medication to change core attention symptoms, and the two are often used together.
