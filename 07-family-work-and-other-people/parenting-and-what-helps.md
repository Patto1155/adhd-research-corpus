# Parent training and school support for ADHD: do they help?

> **Short answer:** Yes, but for different things than many people expect. Parent training improves parenting and slightly reduces children's behaviour problems, including on blinded ratings. Effects on core ADHD symptoms shrink to little or nothing on blinded short-term ratings, though a review of longer follow-ups found small sustained gains. Classroom programmes bring small benefits on disruptive behaviour.

## What the evidence shows

**Rating bias matters a lot here.** In behavioural treatment studies, the people rating the child (usually parents and teachers) often know who received the treatment and have invested in it. Researchers therefore separate *unblinded* ratings from *probably blinded* ones, made by observers who do not know which group a child was in. A 2013 meta-analysis found that with unblinded ratings, behavioural interventions appeared to reduce ADHD symptoms; with probably-blinded ratings, the effect on core symptoms was not significant [1].

**Parent training improves parenting and conduct, even when blinded.** A meta-analysis of randomised trials of behavioural interventions for children with ADHD found that, on probably-blinded measures, effects held for positive parenting (standardised mean difference, SMD, 0.63), negative parenting (0.43) and child conduct problems (0.31) [2]. An SMD of 0.3 is small: by our calculation from that figure, treated and untreated groups overlap by almost 90%. An SMD of 0.6 is moderate, with about 75% overlap by the same calculation.

On unblinded ratings, ADHD symptoms improved by SMD 0.35, along with parents' confidence, children's social skills and schoolwork [2]. Parents' own mental wellbeing did not clearly improve [2].

**In preschool children the pattern is the same.** A meta-analysis of 16 studies (1,003 children aged 2.5 to 6) found moderate effects on parent-rated ADHD symptoms (Hedges' g about 0.51), conduct problems (0.40) and negative parenting (0.63), lasting 3 to 12 months [3]. On independent assessment, only the effect on negative parenting remained significant; there was no significant effect on independently assessed ADHD symptoms [3]. This evidence is why NICE recommends group parent training as the first-line treatment for children under 5, as described in [ADHD medication for children: is it safe and worthwhile?](children-and-medication.md)

**Newer reviews: benefits last for months, and parenting change goes with child change.** A 2023 meta-analysis of 27 randomised trials found small-to-moderate benefits of parent training on children's ADHD symptoms, behaviour problems and parenting about five months after treatment, and, unlike some short-term analyses, no difference between blinded and unblinded ratings of ADHD symptoms at follow-up [4]. A 2022 meta-analysis of 29 trials (2,345 families) found small-to-medium gains in parenting and parents' confidence on both unblinded and probably blinded measures [5]. An analysis of individual data from 19 trials (1,720 children) found that improvements in parenting went together with improvements in children's parent-rated ADHD severity and behaviour, in both directions [6].

These newer results rest mostly on parent reports and were read at abstract level, and how many blinded symptom ratings the follow-up analysis held was not checked [4]. So the two bodies of evidence differ mainly in timing and rater: short-term blinded ratings show little change in core symptoms [1, 2], while longer follow-ups suggest modest change that may hold.

**Classroom programmes reduce disruptive behaviour.** A meta-analysis restricted to randomised trials (19 trials, 18,094 primary-school children) found small benefits of behavioural classroom programmes on disruptive and on-task behaviour, measured by teacher ratings and classroom observation [7]. These trials studied disruptive behaviour in general, many with whole-class programmes, so they are not specific to ADHD.

**Treating a child's ADHD tends to ease parental stress.** Parents of children with ADHD report more stress than other parents, in line with symptom severity; more is on [ADHD in children: how common, did we cause it, and what comes next?](adhd-in-children-and-teenagers.md)

A 2018 meta-analysis of 43 studies found that interventions for children's ADHD were followed by moderate reductions in parenting stress: *d* = 0.50 comparing before and after treatment (33 studies) and *d* = 0.53 comparing treated with untreated groups (9 studies) [9]. By our calculation from those figures, treated and untreated parents' stress scores still overlap by about 79%. Parents rated their own stress and knew whether their child had been treated.

## What is uncertain or debated

**Whether blinded measures are fair.** Blinded observers see a child for short periods and may miss changes that parents see daily, so a null blinded result does not prove there is no change at home. Equally, parent ratings may be inflated by hope and effort. Both views appear in the research literature, and the honest summary is that blinded evidence for core-symptom change is weak.

**Which parts of programmes work.** Studies have tried to identify active ingredients, such as praise, consistent consequences and time-out, but no single component has been firmly established. One analysis linked techniques that change what happens before a behaviour, and rewards for wanted behaviour, with larger gains for parents, though techniques were not tested in isolation [5].

**Long-term effects.** Most trials follow families for months, not years; the longer follow-ups pooled so far averaged about five months [4]. Whether parent training changes the long-term course of ADHD is not known.

**Parents who have ADHD themselves.** ADHD is highly heritable, so many parents in these programmes have ADHD traits too. Whether standard programmes work as well for them has been studied little; no review was found for this page.

**Evidence from secondary school.** Most school studies are in primary-age children. Evidence for teenagers is thinner.

## What this means for you

Parent training is offered because, on average, it improves parenting and reduces children's conflict and defiance, which are often what make family life hardest. Being offered it is not a verdict on your parenting: it is not offered because parenting caused the ADHD, and the evidence on causes points elsewhere. It is less likely than medication to change core attention symptoms, and the two are often used together.

The evidence on causes is on [what does and doesn't cause ADHD](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/what-does-and-doesnt-cause-adhd.md).
