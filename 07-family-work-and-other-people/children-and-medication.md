# ADHD medication for children: is it safe and worthwhile?

> **Short answer:** In short-term trials, ADHD medicines reduce children's symptoms with medium-to-large effects, and in one large trial more than behavioural treatment. In that trial, the advantage faded by three years once families chose their own treatment. One follow-up linked consistent childhood use with about 2.5 cm less adult height; another cohort found no difference. Effects are smaller in preschool children.

## What the evidence shows

**Short-term effects in children are well established.** Network meta-analyses and an umbrella review covering 221 treatment–comparison–outcome combinations find medium-to-large short-term symptom reductions for ADHD medicines in children and adolescents, with methylphenidate preferred as a first choice in children [1][2]. Reviews disagree about how certain this evidence is, not about its size. The general figures and the disagreement are on [how well medication works](../06-treatment-what-works/how-well-medication-works.md).

**The MTA trial: medication beat behaviour therapy at 14 months.** The US Multimodal Treatment Study randomly assigned 579 children aged 7 to 9 to carefully managed medication, intensive behavioural treatment, both combined, or usual community care [3]. At 14 months, medication alone and combined treatment reduced core ADHD symptoms more than behavioural treatment or community care [3].

Combined treatment was not clearly better than medication alone on core symptoms, though it had modest advantages on some other outcomes [3]. Community care did worse than the managed-medication arm even though two-thirds of community children also took medication, which points to the importance of careful dose adjustment and follow-up [3].

</details>

**After the trial, the groups converged.** Once the trial ended, families chose their own treatment. By three years the differences between the original groups were no longer significant, and at eight years the original treatment group did not predict how teenagers were doing; how their symptoms had changed early on did [4].

Teenagers no longer taking medication at eight years were, as a group, doing about as well as those still on it [4]. Because treatment was no longer randomised, these follow-ups cannot show whether medication works long term; children who did worse may have been more likely to stay on it.

**Growth: slower during treatment; the effect on adult height is unsettled.** At about age 25, MTA participants who had taken medication consistently or inconsistently through childhood were about 2.5 cm shorter than those who had taken little or none (2.55 cm, compared with the "negligible use" subgroup) [5]. Compared with classmates without ADHD, the ADHD group as a whole was about 1.3 cm shorter as adults; that compares ADHD with no ADHD, not medication with none [5]. The same comparison found no lower adult symptom severity in the consistently medicated group [5].

Not every study finds a difference in adult height. A US birth-cohort study of 340 children with ADHD and 680 without found that neither ADHD nor stimulant treatment was associated with a difference in final adult height, though treated boys had their growth spurt slightly later [11].

A meta-analysis of 18 studies (4,868 children) of methylphenidate taken for more than six months found small reductions in height and weight scores [6]. The standardised effect sizes were 0.27 and 0.33, meaning about a quarter to a third of the normal spread of heights and weights among children, which the authors judged of possibly minimal clinical impact [6].

Guidelines recommend measuring height and weight before and during treatment. Other safety findings, such as appetite, sleep and heart rate, are on [medication safety](../06-treatment-what-works/medication-safety.md).

**Preschool children respond less.** In a placebo-controlled trial of methylphenidate in children aged 3 to 5, symptom reductions were smaller than in school-age children (effect sizes 0.4–0.8), and only 21% on their best dose reached remission against 13% on placebo [7].

A companion report from the same trial measured growth in 140 of the children, who started out taller and heavier than average [10]. In the 95 who stayed on medication for about a year, growth was about 1.4 cm a year less in height and 1.3 kg a year less in weight than expected for their age (about 20% and 55% below expected growth rates) [10]. This was a comparison with expected growth, not with untreated children, and it does not show what happened to adult height; the authors advised weighing the risk of slower growth against the expected benefits at this age [10].

NICE recommends an ADHD-focused group parent-training programme as the first-line treatment for children under 5, citing limited evidence on medication and concern about long-term effects on growth and development at that age [8]. It also advises that medication should not be offered to any child under 5 without a second opinion from a specialist ADHD service with expertise in young children, ideally a tertiary service [8].

## What is uncertain or debated

**Long-term benefit.** An umbrella review found no high-certainty evidence of long-term benefit for any ADHD treatment in children or adults [2]. Registry studies comparing the same person on and off medication suggest lower rates of injuries and other harms during medicated periods, but they are observational; see [what registries show](../06-treatment-what-works/what-registries-show.md).

**What the height finding means.** The MTA height data come from a naturalistic follow-up, not randomisation, and dose and pattern varied. Whether height is caught up after stopping, and whether drug holidays help, is not settled.

**Certainty grading.** The Cochrane review of methylphenidate in children rates all outcomes as very low certainty, while other groups rate similar data moderate or high [1][9]. The disagreement concerns risk of bias and blinding, not whether the average effect exists.

**Later substance use.** A common worry is that childhood stimulants lead to later drug problems. The evidence on this is on [alcohol, drugs and addiction](../05-what-often-comes-with-it/alcohol-drugs-and-addiction.md).

## What this means for you

The research supports medication as an effective short-term treatment for many children, with side effects that need monitoring, including height. It cannot promise that benefits measured over weeks or months will add up to better adult outcomes, because the long-term trials that would show that have not been done.

Whether to use medication, and starting, pausing or stopping it, is a decision for you and your child to make with their prescriber. More is on [Can these pages tell me what to do about my ADHD?](../08-reading-this-well/not-medical-advice.md)
