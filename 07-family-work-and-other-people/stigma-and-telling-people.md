# ADHD stigma and shame: should I tell people?

> **Short answer:** Research cannot tell you whether to tell people, or how it will go. It shows that negative attitudes are common, that the stigma adults with ADHD notice most is doubt that it is real, and that many expect to be treated worse if others know. In one written-scenario experiment, disclosing in advance softened readers' reactions.

## What the evidence shows

**Stigma comes in several kinds.** A 2012 review of the research on children, teenagers and adults set out three: *public stigma* (negative beliefs held by others), *self-stigma* (taking those beliefs on about yourself) and *courtesy stigma* (stigma attached to family members) [1]. It linked stigma to delayed treatment and support, narrower social lives, and lower self-esteem [1].

**Public attitudes are generally negative.** A 2022 systematic review of 10 recent studies from several countries concluded that community attitudes towards people with ADHD are mostly unfavourable [2]. The reviewers suggested better public knowledge of mental health might help, but did not find trials showing that it does [2].

**Many people still know little about ADHD.** In a US national interview survey published in 2007, about two-thirds of adults said they had heard of ADHD, but fewer than half could say anything specific about it [3]. The survey also asked whether ADHD is a "real" disease, but that result was not available in the records checked for this page.

**The public tends to picture adult ADHD as worse than it is.** In a German study, 105 people without ADHD filled in standard ADHD questionnaires as if they had it [4]. Compared with the answers of 98 adults who did have ADHD, they overestimated problems, most strongly with hyperactivity, impulsivity, risky behaviour and functioning at work and school [4]. The authors read this as a sign of stigmatising beliefs; people with ADHD symptoms of their own gave more accurate pictures [4].

**Expecting discrimination is very common.** A German study of 104 adults with ADHD found that 23% had high self-stigma, 89% expected to be discriminated against in daily life, and 69% perceived public stigma [5]. The stereotype they perceived most often was doubt that ADHD is a genuine disorder [5].

**Self-stigma goes with worse wellbeing.** In the same study, self-stigma and expected discrimination were linked with more severe symptoms, more distress, lower self-esteem, more impairment and lower quality of life [5]. This is a single snapshot: stigma could worsen wellbeing, people who are struggling more could feel more stigmatised, or both.

**Stigma makes people less likely to tell, at work and elsewhere.** In a survey of 166 working adults with ADHD, those more conscious of stigma were less likely to disclose before problems arose, and those who felt psychologically safe at work (able to speak up without fear) were more likely to [6]. Disclosing early was linked to feeling they were thriving [6].

A small 2026 survey of young adults aged 18 to 29 looked at how they decide whom to tell [7]; its figures were seen only in a magazine summary and are not quoted here. Both studies were snapshots of self-selected volunteers.

**Disclosure can soften reactions, in one experiment.** A vignette study of 306 young adults found that when a person's ADHD was disclosed in advance, readers expressed much less socially rejecting attitudes towards them (*d* = −0.95, a large difference) [8]. The study was published in 2007, so attitudes may have changed since. Reactions to a written description are not the same as reactions from a real manager or friend.

## What is uncertain or debated

**Where shame starts.** It is plausible that years of criticism for forgetting, lateness or restlessness, before anyone named ADHD, feed self-stigma. Interview studies of late-diagnosed adults describe exactly this, but no study found here measured how much it contributes; see [after the diagnosis](../01-is-this-real/after-the-diagnosis.md).

**Whether disclosure helps or harms on balance.** No study followed people who did and did not disclose at work to compare what happened next. The outcome probably depends heavily on the workplace, the person told and the country's legal protections.

**What reduces stigma.** No trial of an intervention to reduce public or self-stigma about ADHD was found for this page.

**Most studies are small and Western.** The studies on this page come from Germany and the US, and the wider literature is mostly European and North American. How stigma differs by culture, ethnicity or gender is barely studied; evidence on differences in who gets diagnosed is on [how diagnosis works](../01-is-this-real/how-diagnosis-works.md).

## What this means for you

If you feel ashamed, the research suggests that much of that feeling has roots outside you: in common public beliefs that ADHD is not real or is worse than it is, and in an expectation of being judged that most adults with ADHD in one study shared. That does not make the feeling go away, but it changes what it is evidence of.

Whether to tell an employer, friend or family member is your decision. The research suggests people disclose more where they feel safe to speak up, and one survey linked early disclosure with feeling they were thriving; it cannot predict any one conversation. What the law protects depends on your country, which these pages do not cover.
