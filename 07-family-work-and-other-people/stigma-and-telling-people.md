# Why do I feel ashamed of having ADHD, and should I tell people?

> **Short answer:** Negative public attitudes towards people with ADHD are common and well documented, and the most common one adults with ADHD notice is doubt that ADHD is real. Many adults with ADHD expect to be treated worse if others find out, and some come to believe the stereotypes about themselves, which researchers call self-stigma. Self-stigma goes with lower self-esteem and more distress, though studies cannot yet show which comes first. Research on telling employers, friends or family is small: it suggests stigma makes people less likely to disclose, and that disclosure can sometimes soften others' reactions, but it cannot tell you whether disclosing will go well for you.

## What the evidence shows

**Stigma comes in several kinds.** A 2012 review of the research on children, teenagers and adults set out three: *public stigma* (negative beliefs held by others), *self-stigma* (taking those beliefs on about yourself) and *courtesy stigma* (stigma attached to family members) [1]. <!-- CHECK: Mueller 2012 details came from its listing in another paper's references, not its own record --> It linked stigma to delayed treatment and support, narrower social lives, and lower self-esteem [1].

**Public attitudes are generally negative.** A 2022 systematic review of 10 recent studies from several countries concluded that community attitudes towards people with ADHD are mostly unfavourable [2]. The reviewers suggested better public knowledge of mental health might help, but did not find trials showing that it does [2].

**Many people still know little about ADHD.** In a 2002 US national survey of 1,139 adults, about 64% had heard of ADHD and could say something about it [3]. The survey also asked whether ADHD is a "real" disease, but the result was not available in the search record for this page. <!-- CHECK: McLeod 2007 headline figures on "real disease" and treatment attitudes are in the abstract or full text; add or cut. **The public tends to picture adult ADHD as worse than it is.** In a German study, 105 people without ADHD filled in standard ADHD questionnaires as if they had it [4]. Compared with the answers of 98 adults who did have ADHD, they overestimated problems, most strongly with hyperactivity, impulsivity, risky behaviour and functioning at work and school [4]. The authors read this as a sign of stigmatising beliefs; people with ADHD symptoms of their own gave more accurate pictures [4].

**Expecting discrimination is very common.** A German study of 104 adults with ADHD found that 23% had high self-stigma, 89% expected to be discriminated against in daily life, and 69% perceived public stigma [5]. The stereotype they perceived most often was doubt that ADHD is a genuine disorder [5]. A summary of the study reports that people expected the most discrimination from employers, colleagues, neighbours and teachers, and less from health professionals, family and friends [5]. <!-- CHECK: the "who they expect discrimination from" detail is from a blog summary (adhdinadults.com), not the abstract -->

**Self-stigma goes with worse wellbeing.** In the same study, self-stigma and expected discrimination were linked with more severe symptoms, more distress, lower self-esteem, more impairment and lower quality of life [5]. This is a single snapshot: stigma could worsen wellbeing, people who are struggling more could feel more stigmatised, or both.

**Stigma makes people less likely to tell, at work and elsewhere.** In a survey of 166 working adults with ADHD, those more conscious of stigma were less likely to disclose before problems arose, and those who felt psychologically safe at work (able to speak up without fear) were more likely to [6]. Disclosing early was linked to feeling they were thriving [6]. In a survey of 60 people aged 18 to 29, 14% rarely or never told others, and about three-quarters of those who told did so to help others understand their behaviour [7]. <!-- CHECK: Mazur 2026 figures are from a Simply Psychology summary; confirm against abstract --> Both studies were snapshots of self-selected volunteers.

**Disclosure can soften reactions, in one experiment.** A vignette study of 306 young adults found that when a person's ADHD was disclosed in advance, readers expressed much less socially rejecting attitudes towards them (*d* = −0.95, a large difference) [8]. <!-- CHECK: authors, journal, year and identifier of this study not captured; seen only as a University of Memphis repository record titled "Disclosure of Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder may minimize risk of social rejection" --> Reactions to a written description are not the same as reactions from a real manager or friend.

## What is uncertain or debated

**Where shame starts.** It is plausible that years of criticism for forgetting, lateness or restlessness, before anyone named ADHD, feed self-stigma. Interview studies of late-diagnosed adults describe exactly this ([after the diagnosis](../01-is-this-real/after-the-diagnosis.md)), but no study found here measured how much it contributes.

**Whether disclosure helps or harms on balance.** No study followed people who did and did not disclose at work to compare what happened next. The outcome probably depends heavily on the workplace, the person told and the country's legal protections.

**What reduces stigma.** No trial of an intervention to reduce public or self-stigma about ADHD was found for this page.

**Most studies are small and Western.** The studies on this page come from Germany and the US, and the wider literature is mostly European and North American. How stigma differs by culture, ethnicity or gender is barely studied; see [how diagnosis works](../01-is-this-real/how-diagnosis-works.md) for evidence on differences in who gets diagnosed.

## What this means for you

If you feel ashamed, the research suggests that much of that feeling has roots outside you: in common public beliefs that ADHD is not real or is worse than it is, and in an expectation of being judged that most adults with ADHD in one study shared. That does not make the feeling go away, but it changes what it is evidence of. Whether to tell an employer, friend or family member is your decision; the research suggests it tends to go better where people feel safe to speak up, and it cannot predict any one conversation. What the law protects depends on your country, which is outside this corpus.
