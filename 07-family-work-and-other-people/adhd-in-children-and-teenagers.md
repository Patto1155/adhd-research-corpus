# My child has ADHD. How common is it, did we cause it, and what happens as they grow up?

> **Short answer:** ADHD affects roughly 5–8% of children worldwide, which means one or two children in a typical class. It is mostly explained by genetic differences, and the evidence does not support parenting, diet or ordinary family stress as the cause, although how a family responds can shape how things go. Most children with ADHD still have some symptoms in adulthood, but only a minority still meet full criteria at 25, and many move in and out of the diagnosis over the years. Hyperactivity usually fades first; inattention tends to last longer.

## What the evidence shows

**It is common.** A 2023 meta-analysis of 61 studies estimated ADHD in 7.6% of children aged 3 to 12 and 5.6% of teenagers [1]. An earlier, widely cited meta-analysis using standard criteria put children and adolescents at 5.9–7.1% [2]. Estimates vary mostly because studies measure ADHD differently, not because rates differ between countries [3].

**Parenting does not cause ADHD.** Twin studies find that genetic differences explain roughly three-quarters of the variation in ADHD between people, and the environment that siblings share at home explains very little [4]. That shared environment includes parenting style. Several pregnancy exposures once blamed for ADHD, such as smoking and paracetamol, largely lose their link to it when studies compare siblings within the same family, which points to shared family genes rather than the exposure. The evidence and its limits are on [what does and doesn't cause ADHD](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/what-does-and-doesnt-cause-adhd.md).

**Family life still matters for how things go.** Parent training improves parenting and reduces children's oppositional and conduct problems on independent ratings, even though it has little effect on core ADHD symptoms when raters do not know who was treated ([parenting and what helps](parenting-and-what-helps.md)). Parents of children with ADHD report more parenting stress than other parents, and stress rises with the severity of the child's symptoms [5]. Stress here is a response to the child's difficulties, not a cause of them; parents of children referred for other conditions report similar stress [5].

**Symptoms usually lessen, but often do not disappear.** In a meta-analysis of follow-up studies, about 15% of children with ADHD still met full criteria at 25; about 65% did if people with continuing impairing symptoms below the full threshold were counted [6]. In the US MTA study, which followed 558 children into their mid-20s, most did not follow a straight line; symptoms waxed and waned, and many who reached full remission later had symptoms return [7]. The full numbers are on [how ADHD changes over life](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/how-adhd-changes-over-life.md).

**Girls are often recognised later.** In Denmark, new diagnoses peaked at age 8 in boys and 17 in girls [8], and in Sweden females were diagnosed about four years later than males on average ([women and girls](../01-is-this-real/women-and-girls.md)).

**The teenage years bring new risks.** On average, adolescents and young adults with ADHD have higher rates of injuries, road crashes and substance use than their peers. These are group averages with absolute risks that are smaller than headlines suggest, and several are lower during periods on medication in registry studies. See [the hard statistics in context](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/the-hard-statistics-in-context.md), [what registries show](../06-treatment-what-works/what-registries-show.md) and [alcohol, drugs and addiction](../05-what-often-comes-with-it/alcohol-drugs-and-addiction.md).

## What is uncertain or debated

**Predicting one child's course.** No test or score predicts whether a particular child's ADHD will persist. In the MTA, how symptoms changed in the first few years was a better guide to later functioning than which treatment a child had received [9].

**Diagnosis in the youngest children.** Children who are young for their school year are diagnosed more often, which suggests some normal immaturity is labelled ADHD ([more diagnoses than ever](../01-is-this-real/more-diagnoses-than-ever.md)). Diagnosis before school age is harder still, and guidelines are more cautious with treatment at that age ([children and medication](children-and-medication.md)).

**Whether "fluctuating" is the norm.** A reanalysis of the MTA data found fewer participants with a fluctuating course than the original report, so how common the in-and-out pattern is remains debated. <!-- CHECK: reanalysis cited in scoping item L2 without full citation -->

**Inheritance in the family.** Because ADHD is highly heritable, a parent may recognise their own traits in their child. There is no genetic test, and no checked figure was found for the chance that a sibling or child of someone with ADHD will have it; see [genes and inheritance](../02-whats-happening-in-my-brain/genes-and-inheritance.md).

## What this means for you

The research does not support the idea that you caused your child's ADHD. It does support the idea that what happens next can be influenced: by support at home and school, and by treatment where appropriate. Expect change over time rather than a fixed trajectory, and expect that change may not be smooth.
