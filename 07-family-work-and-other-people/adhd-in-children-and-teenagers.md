# ADHD in children: how common, did we cause it, and what comes next?

> **Short answer:** ADHD affects roughly 5–8% of children worldwide, one or two in a typical class, and the evidence does not support parenting as the cause; twin studies link most of the variation between people to genes. Most children still have some symptoms in adulthood, but about 15% meet full criteria at 25. Some move in and out of the diagnosis.

## What the evidence shows

**It is common.** A 2023 meta-analysis of 61 studies estimated ADHD in 7.6% of children aged 3 to 12 and 5.6% of teenagers aged 12 to 18 [1]. An earlier, widely cited meta-analysis of 86 studies using standard criteria found similar pooled rates whether ADHD was judged from parent ratings, teacher ratings or a full diagnostic assessment [2]; its exact range was not checked for this page. Estimates vary mostly because studies measure ADHD differently, not because rates differ between countries [3].

**Parenting does not appear to cause ADHD.** Twin studies find that genetic differences explain roughly three-quarters of the variation in ADHD between people [4], and parenting style and sugar do not appear to cause it; whether ordinary family stress plays a part was not checked for this page. Several pregnancy exposures once blamed for ADHD, such as smoking and paracetamol, largely lose their link to it when studies compare siblings within the same family, which points to shared family genes rather than the exposure. The evidence and its limits are on [what does and doesn't cause ADHD](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/what-does-and-doesnt-cause-adhd.md).

**Family life still matters for how things go.** Parent training improves parenting and reduces children's oppositional and conduct problems on independent ratings, even though it has little effect on core ADHD symptoms when raters do not know who was treated. The detail is on [Parent training and school support for ADHD: do they help?](parenting-and-what-helps.md)

Parents of children with ADHD report more parenting stress than other parents, and stress rises with the severity of the child's symptoms [5]. Parents of children referred for other conditions report similar levels of stress [5].

**Symptoms usually lessen, but often do not disappear.** In a meta-analysis of follow-up studies, about 15% of children with ADHD still met full criteria at 25; about 65% did if people with continuing impairing symptoms below the full threshold were counted [6]. Hyperactivity is often described as fading first and inattention as lasting longer, though that pattern was not checked against a source for this page.

In the US MTA study, which followed 558 children into their mid-20s, most did not follow a straight line: symptoms waxed and waned, and many who reached full remission later had symptoms return [7]. The full numbers are on [how ADHD changes over life](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/how-adhd-changes-over-life.md).

**Girls are often recognised later.** In Denmark, new diagnoses peaked at age 8 in boys and 17 in girls [8], and in Stockholm County, Sweden, females were diagnosed about four years later than males on average [11]. More is on [women and girls](../01-is-this-real/women-and-girls.md).

**The teenage years bring new risks.** On average, adolescents and young adults with ADHD have higher rates of injuries, road crashes and substance use than their peers. These are group averages with absolute risks that are smaller than headlines suggest; see [the hard statistics in context](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/the-hard-statistics-in-context.md).

Several of these risks are lower during periods on medication in registry studies. The evidence on substance use is on [alcohol, drugs and addiction](../05-what-often-comes-with-it/alcohol-drugs-and-addiction.md).

## What is uncertain or debated

**Predicting one child's course.** No test or score predicts whether a particular child's ADHD will persist. In the MTA, how symptoms changed in the first few years was a better guide to later functioning than which treatment a child had received [9].

**Diagnosis in the youngest children.** Children who are young for their school year are diagnosed more often, which suggests some normal immaturity is labelled ADHD. More is on [more diagnoses than ever](../01-is-this-real/more-diagnoses-than-ever.md).

Diagnosis before school age is harder still, and guidelines are more cautious with treatment at that age. More is on [ADHD medication for children: is it safe and worthwhile?](children-and-medication.md)

**Whether "fluctuating" is the norm.** A study of three other US cohorts, counting a course as fluctuating after two or more switches in and out of diagnosis, found it in 17–29% of young people with ADHD, fewer than in the MTA, and never as the largest group [10]. How common the in-and-out pattern is therefore remains debated.

**Inheritance in the family.** Because ADHD is highly heritable, a parent may recognise their own traits in their child. There is no genetic test, and no checked figure was found for the chance that a sibling or child of someone with ADHD will have it; see [genes and inheritance](../02-whats-happening-in-my-brain/genes-and-inheritance.md).

## What this means for you

The research does not support the idea that you caused your child's ADHD. It does support the idea that what happens next can be influenced: by support at home and school, and by treatment where appropriate. Expect change over time rather than a fixed trajectory, and expect that change may not be smooth.
