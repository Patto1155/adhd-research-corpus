# Is ADHD genetic, and will my children have it?

> **Short answer:** ADHD is strongly inherited: twin studies put heritability at about 74%, meaning most of the *variation* in ADHD across a population links to genetic differences. A child of a parent with ADHD probably has a raised chance, not a certainty; no figure specific to parents was found. Many tiny-effect variants contribute; there is no "ADHD gene" and no genetic test.

## What the evidence shows

**Twin studies: heritability of about 74%.** Heritability is the share of the differences between people in a population that is linked to genetic differences between them. Averaged across 37 twin studies, the heritability of ADHD or of its symptoms is about 74% [1].

**Heritability describes a population, not a person.** A heritability of 74% does *not* mean that 74% of one person's ADHD comes from genes, and it does not mean ADHD is fixed or untouched by environment. ADHD is caused by thousands of common genetic variants, each with a tiny effect, plus non-inherited factors. [How to read the evidence](../08-reading-this-well/how-to-read-the-evidence.md) explains heritability in more detail.

**Adult ADHD is still strongly heritable when it is measured well.** Early studies that asked adults to rate their own symptoms gave much lower heritability estimates. A Swedish national register study of twins used clinical diagnoses instead: heritability was 0.88 overall and 0.72 in adults: lower in adults, but far above the earlier self-rated estimates [2]. The authors concluded that the earlier low adult figures were mostly an artefact of self-rating [2].

**Family risk rises with how closely related you are.** A Swedish register study followed 1,656,943 people born between 1985 and 2006 [3]. Compared with the relatives of people without ADHD, the chance of an ADHD diagnosis (measured as a hazard ratio, roughly "how many times more often, at any given time") was:

| Relative with ADHD | Hazard ratio |
|---|---|
| Identical twin | 70.45 |
| Non-identical twin | 8.44 |
| Full sibling | 8.27 |
| Maternal half-sibling | 2.86 |
| Paternal half-sibling | 2.31 |
| Full cousin | 2.24 |
| Half cousin | 1.47 |

The pattern tracks shared genes, though the authors concluded that a small amount of shared family environment also contributes [3]. ADHD that persisted into adulthood showed stronger family clustering: the hazard ratio for full siblings was 11.49 when the relative's ADHD continued past age 18, and 4.68 when it was diagnosed only in childhood [3].

**These are relative figures, not how many people.** The abstract does not give absolute risks, and no checked absolute figure for how many siblings are diagnosed was found for this page, so none is given here. A raised relative risk is not a certainty, and no checked figure was found specifically for children of an affected *parent* (rather than a sibling).

**Many genes, each with a tiny effect.** The largest genome-wide association study (GWAS: a scan of the whole genome comparing people with and without a condition) included 38,691 people with ADHD and 186,843 without. It found 27 regions of the genome and 76 candidate genes linked to ADHD [4]. These genes are expressed particularly during early brain development, and the risk is enriched in several types of brain cell, including midbrain dopamine neurons [4].

An earlier genome-wide study of 20,183 people with ADHD, the first to find clearly linked regions (12 of them), found that the variants linked to a diagnosis were the same ones linked to ADHD traits in the general population [5]. Its authors concluded that a diagnosis is the extreme end of continuous, heritable traits [5].

**Most of these variants are shared with other conditions.** An estimated 84–98% of the variants that influence ADHD also influence other psychiatric conditions [4], which helps explain why ADHD so often comes with anxiety, depression or autism.

**Measured DNA captures less than twin studies.** The 2019 genome-wide study estimated that common variants together account for about 22% of the variation in liability to ADHD (a "SNP heritability" of 0.216, assuming 5% of people have ADHD) [5]. A 2019 review described this as about a third of the twin-study heritability [1]. <!-- CHECK: the SNP heritability in the full text of source 4 (2023) was not seen; update if it differs -->

**Rare genetic changes play a smaller part.** Large, rare deletions or duplications of DNA (copy number variants, CNVs) were about twice as common in 366 children with ADHD as in 1,047 without ADHD (0.156 vs 0.075 per person) [6]. These overlapped with regions linked to autism and schizophrenia. They are found in only a minority of people with ADHD.

**The old "dopamine genes" have small effects.** Before GWAS, research focused on single candidate genes such as the dopamine transporter gene (DAT1, also called SLC6A3) and the dopamine D4 receptor gene (DRD4). A 2009 meta-analysis found associations for these and several other genes, with significant variation between studies [7]. A checked figure for the size of each association was not found for this page, and these genes are not tests and do not explain ADHD on their own.

## What is uncertain or debated

- **The "missing" heritability.** Twin studies give about 74%; measured DNA explains much less. Rare variants, gene–environment interplay and the assumptions of twin studies may each account for part of the gap.
- **Genes acting through the family environment.** Some of what looks like genetic influence may work through the home that parents' genes help create (an *indirect genetic effect*), not through the child's own DNA. In about 24,700 Norwegian parent–child trios, mother-rated hyperactivity at age 3 was linked more to parents' genes acting this way (9.6% of the variation) than to the child's own genes (1.3%); for inattention the figures were 6.7% and 4.8% [8]. These were toddler traits rated by mothers, not ADHD diagnoses, and twin studies do not separate the two kinds of effect.
- **Heritability is not destiny.** High heritability is compatible with environment mattering a great deal for how ADHD plays out. Genes and environment are also tangled: parents pass on both genes and surroundings.
- **Polygenic scores cannot diagnose.** A polygenic score adds up a person's many small genetic risks. At present it explains only a small share of who has ADHD and cannot tell an individual whether they, or their child, have or will develop it. (A checked figure for how much it explains was not found for this page.)
- **Recurrence risk for a parent's children.** No checked figure was found. The sibling data above are the closest guide.

## What this means for you

If you have ADHD, there is a reasonable chance a close relative does too, and your children have a higher chance than average. Most of the genetic risk is spread thinly across thousands of common variants that everyone carries in some combination, so ADHD sits at one end of a range of traits found throughout the population.

No genetic test can predict ADHD, and heritability says nothing about blame. The evidence on pregnancy exposures and parenting is in [what does and doesn't cause ADHD](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/what-does-and-doesnt-cause-adhd.md).
