# Is ADHD genetic, and will my children have it?

> **Short answer:** ADHD is one of the most strongly inherited conditions in psychiatry. Twin studies put its heritability at about 74%, which means most of the *variation* in ADHD traits across a population is linked to genetic differences between people. It is caused by thousands of common genetic variants, each with a tiny effect, plus non-inherited factors. There is no single "ADHD gene" and no genetic test. Children of a parent with ADHD have a raised chance of ADHD, not a certainty.

## What the evidence shows

**Twin studies: heritability of about 74%.** Heritability is the share of the differences between people in a population that is linked to genetic differences between them. Averaged across 37 twin studies, the heritability of ADHD or of its symptoms is about 74% [1]. The same review found that the environment family members share (the same home, parenting style or income) explains little of the variation [1]. A heritability of 74% does *not* mean that 74% of one person's ADHD comes from genes, and it does not mean ADHD is fixed or untouched by environment. It describes a population, not a person. [How to read the evidence](../08-reading-this-well/how-to-read-the-evidence.md) explains this in more detail.

**Adult ADHD is just as heritable when it is measured well.** Early studies that asked adults to rate their own symptoms gave much lower heritability estimates. A Swedish register study of 59,514 twins used clinical diagnoses instead. Heritability was 0.88 overall and 0.72 in adults, and the authors concluded that the earlier low adult figures were mostly an artefact of self-rating [2].

**Family risk rises with how closely related you are.** A Swedish register study followed 1,656,943 people born between 1985 and 2006 [3]. Compared with people who had no relative with ADHD, the chance of an ADHD diagnosis (measured as a hazard ratio, roughly "how many times more often, at any given time") was:

| Relative with ADHD | Hazard ratio |
|---|---|
| Identical twin | 70.45 |
| Non-identical twin | 8.44 |
| Full sibling | 8.27 |
| Maternal half-sibling | 2.86 |
| Paternal half-sibling | 2.31 |
| Full cousin | 2.24 |
| Half cousin | 1.47 |

The pattern tracks shared genes, with a small extra contribution from shared environment. ADHD that persisted into adulthood showed stronger family clustering [3]. These are relative figures. **Many genes, each with a tiny effect.** The largest genome-wide association study (GWAS: a scan of the whole genome comparing people with and without a condition) included 38,691 people with ADHD and 186,843 without. It found 27 regions of the genome and 76 candidate genes linked to ADHD [4]. These genes are expressed particularly during early brain development, and the risk is enriched in several types of brain cell, including midbrain dopamine neurons [4]. An estimated 84–98% of the variants that influence ADHD also influence other psychiatric conditions [4], which helps explain why ADHD so often comes with anxiety, depression or autism. **Rare genetic changes play a smaller part.** Large, rare deletions or duplications of DNA (copy number variants, CNVs) were about twice as common in 366 children with ADHD as in 1,047 controls (0.156 vs 0.075 per person) [5]. These overlapped with regions linked to autism and schizophrenia. They are found in only a minority of people with ADHD.

**The old "dopamine genes" have small effects.** Before GWAS, research focused on single candidate genes such as the dopamine transporter gene (DAT1, also called SLC6A3) and the dopamine D4 receptor gene (DRD4). ## What is uncertain or debated

- **The "missing" heritability.** Twin studies give about 74%; measured DNA explains much less. Rare variants, gene–environment interplay and the assumptions of twin studies may each account for part of the gap.
- **Heritability is not destiny.** High heritability is compatible with environment mattering a great deal for how ADHD plays out. Genes and environment are also tangled: parents pass on both genes and surroundings.
- **Polygenic scores cannot diagnose.** A polygenic score adds up a person's many small genetic risks. At present it explains only a small share of who has ADHD and cannot tell an individual whether they, or their child, have or will develop it [4]. <!-- CHECK: exact variance explained by ADHD polygenic scores was not found in the Demontis 2023 abstract. -->
- **Recurrence risk for a parent's children.** No checked figure was found. The sibling data above are the closest guide.

## What this means for you

If you have ADHD, there is a reasonable chance a close relative does too, and your children have a higher chance than average. Most of the genetic risk is spread thinly across thousands of common variants that everyone carries in some combination, so ADHD sits at one end of a range of traits found throughout the population. No genetic test can predict it, and heritability says nothing about blame.
