# Why is my ADHD so different from someone else's?

> **Short answer:** Because ADHD does not seem to be one fault in one place: different combinations of genes, brain development and thinking styles appear to lead to similar symptoms. No single brain difference or test weakness is found in everyone with ADHD, some people score normally on lab tests, and no brain scan can yet sort individuals reliably.

## What the evidence shows

**The diagnosis itself allows many combinations.** ADHD is diagnosed from lists of inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive symptoms, and a person needs a set number from one or both lists, so two people can meet criteria while sharing few symptoms. The diagnostic manual describes the result as predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, or combined presentation, and a person's presentation can change over time [1]. The criteria are set out in [How is ADHD diagnosed, and why is there no test?](../01-is-this-real/how-diagnosis-works.md)

**Executive weaknesses are common but not universal.** A meta-analysis (a study that statistically combines earlier studies) of 83 studies, covering 3,734 people with ADHD and 2,969 without, found average weaknesses in executive functions (the mental skills used to plan, hold information in mind and stop an action) [2]. The differences were medium-sized (*d* = 0.46 to 0.69, where Cohen's *d* measures the gap between two group averages in units of how spread out people are) [2]. By this page's own calculation, not a figure from the paper, the two groups' scores overlap by roughly 73–82%.

**The authors concluded that executive weaknesses are "neither necessary nor sufficient" to cause all ADHD.** Not everyone with ADHD shows them on tests [2]. A companion review argued that executive weaknesses may mark only a subgroup, and proposed defining an "executive deficit" subtype for research [3]. A checked figure for what share of people with ADHD show executive impairment was not found for this page.

**More than one route can lead to ADHD.** The "dual pathway" model proposes at least two partly separate routes: one through executive difficulties, and one through motivation, especially a strong dislike of waiting (delay aversion) linked to the brain's reward systems [4]. A later study of 71 children with ADHD found that difficulties with timing formed a third, separate component, and that having one difficulty did not make having another more likely than chance; the authors called for replication in larger samples [5]. More is in [time, waiting and rewards](../03-why-i-think-and-feel-this-way/time-waiting-and-rewards.md).

**ADHD's variety sits inside ordinary human variety.** A study of children with and without ADHD sorted them by their profiles on thinking tasks [6]. Children *without* ADHD also fell into distinct subgroups, and the subgroups found in ADHD largely mirrored them; the authors described ADHD heterogeneity as "nested" within normal variation [6]. Put plainly, the ways people with ADHD differ from each other are much like the ways everyone differs.

**Shared genes blur the edges.** An estimated 84–98% of the genetic variants that influence ADHD also influence other psychiatric conditions [7]. So the same genetic background can show up as different mixtures of traits, as [Is ADHD genetic, and will my children have it?](genes-and-inheritance.md) explains.

**Some features are common but not specific to ADHD.** One of the most consistent lab findings is that people with ADHD respond with more variable reaction times: a meta-analysis of 319 studies found this in children and adolescents (*g* = 0.76) and adults (*g* = 0.46) compared with people without ADHD [8]. But children with ADHD were only slightly more variable than children with *other* clinical conditions (*g* = 0.25 [8]; about 90% overlap by this page's calculation), and adolescents and adults with ADHD could not be told apart from other clinical groups [8]. A feature can be typical of ADHD without being unique to it.

### Why a brain scan cannot sort individuals

Group differences in brain activity and chemistry are covered on [Is ADHD caused by low dopamine?](dopamine-noradrenaline-and-myths.md), and in brain structure on [Does ADHD mean my brain is damaged or "three years behind"?](brain-structure-and-development.md). This section explains why those average differences do not turn into a test for one person.

**Imaging studies do not converge on one region.** A meta-analysis of 96 structural and functional imaging studies in young people (1,914 participants) found no brain region with consistent differences in its main analysis [9]. The authors suggested this may reflect differences between patient groups and methods, or that ADHD depends more on how brain networks interact than on single regions [9].

**Normative modelling shows each person differs in their own way.** Normative modelling builds a reference chart of typical brain measures for each age, like a height-for-age growth chart, then asks where each individual sits on it. In 153 adults with ADHD and 146 without, individual deviations from the chart were frequent in ADHD, but they rarely lined up: only in a few brain locations did more than 2% of the ADHD group share the same deviation [10]. The group did show smaller average grey matter in the cerebellum and hippocampus, yet individuals "only marginally reflected" these group differences [10].

**The same pattern appears across other conditions.** A normative-modelling study of 1,294 people with one of six conditions, including ADHD, and 1,465 without found that a person's brain-volume deviations hit the same region in fewer than 7% of people with the same diagnosis [11]. The deviations were more consistent at the level of whole networks than of single regions [11].

**Claims of highly accurate scan-based diagnosis have not held up.** A review of 69 studies that used brain imaging to classify people as having ADHD or not found that some reported accuracies were inflated by "circular analysis" (testing a model on the same data used to build it) [12]. In studies that kept their test data properly separate, accuracy ranged from 60% to 80%, and smaller studies tended to report higher accuracy, a sign of bias [12]. For comparison, 50% is what a coin toss would achieve when the two groups are the same size.

**In one international competition, a model with no scan data won.** In the 2011 ADHD-200 competition, teams used resting-state scans (brain activity recorded while lying still) from 973 young people to sort them into ADHD types or no ADHD [13]. The most accurate entry, at 62.5%, ignored the scans and used only age, sex, handedness, IQ and the site where data were collected; none of the same team's scan-based methods did as well [13].

**Brain-based subtypes are being tried but are not yet usable.** A 2026 study combined normative modelling and clustering of brain-structure scans in 446 children with ADHD and 708 without, and described three "biotypes" with different symptom profiles, which it reported replicating in a second group of 554 children with ADHD [14]. A review of subtyping research judged that an emotionally dysregulated profile is closest to being useful clinically, with cognitive and brain-based profiles less developed [15].

## What is uncertain or debated

- **Whether ADHD should be split into biological subtypes.** Researchers have tried to define subgroups by brain scans, test profiles or genetics [14, 15]. None has yet produced subtypes that have been shown, in independent groups, to predict course or treatment response well enough for clinical use.
- **How much heterogeneity is in the person and how much is in the measurement.** Lab tests and questionnaires often disagree, and some variation reflects which test or scanner was used [9, 12]. More is in [executive function and working memory](../03-why-i-think-and-feel-this-way/executive-function-and-working-memory.md).
- **Whether better methods will close the gap.** Larger samples and normative charts may yet find useful individual markers, but so far the larger and more careful the study, the smaller the differences tend to look [12].
- **Most of this evidence comes from children.** Adult subgroup research is thinner, and the normative-modelling study in ADHD adults [10] was modest in size.

## What this means for you

If your ADHD does not look like a friend's, or like the descriptions you have read online, that fits the evidence rather than counting against your diagnosis. It also means no single explanation (dopamine, a smaller region, a working-memory deficit) is likely to describe you completely. A normal result on one test or scan does not rule ADHD out, and an unusual result does not rule it in.
