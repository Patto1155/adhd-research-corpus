# Why is my ADHD so different from someone else's?

> **Short answer:** Because ADHD is not one fault in one place: many combinations of genes, brain development and thinking styles can lead to the same symptoms. No single brain difference or test weakness is found in everyone with ADHD, and many people with ADHD score normally on lab tests. Two people can share a diagnosis and have quite different underlying profiles.

## What the evidence shows

**The diagnosis itself allows many combinations.** ADHD is diagnosed from lists of inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive symptoms, and a person needs a set number from one or both lists, so two people can meet criteria while sharing few symptoms. The diagnostic manual describes the result as predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, or combined presentation, and a person's presentation can change over time [8]. The criteria are set out in [How is ADHD diagnosed, and why is there no test?](../01-is-this-real/how-diagnosis-works.md)

**Executive weaknesses are common but not universal.** A meta-analysis of 83 studies, covering 3,734 people with ADHD and 2,969 without, found average weaknesses in executive functions (the mental skills used to plan, hold information in mind and stop an action) [1]. The differences were medium-sized (*d* = 0.46 to 0.69 [1]; by this page's own calculation, not a figure from the paper, the two groups' scores overlap by roughly 73–82%). The authors concluded that executive weaknesses are "neither necessary nor sufficient" to cause all ADHD: not everyone with ADHD has them on tests [1].

**Executive weaknesses may mark only a subgroup.** A companion review argued this, and proposed defining an "executive deficit" subtype for research [2]. A checked figure for what share of people with ADHD show executive impairment was not found for this page.

**More than one route can lead to ADHD.** The "dual pathway" model proposes at least two partly separate routes: one through executive difficulties, and one through motivation, especially a strong dislike of waiting (delay aversion) linked to the brain's reward systems [3]. Later work has suggested a third route through timing difficulties. These are models, supported by studies showing the problems are partly separable, not settled facts [3]; more is in [time, waiting and rewards](../03-why-i-think-and-feel-this-way/time-waiting-and-rewards.md).

**ADHD's variety sits inside ordinary human variety.** A study of children with and without ADHD sorted them by their profiles on thinking tasks [4]. Children *without* ADHD also fell into distinct subgroups, and the subgroups found in ADHD largely mirrored them; the authors described ADHD heterogeneity as "nested" within normal variation [4]. Put plainly, the ways people with ADHD differ from each other are much like the ways everyone differs.

**Brain-imaging studies do not converge on one region.** Across 96 imaging studies in young people, the main analysis found no brain region with consistent differences, and the authors suggested ADHD may depend more on how brain networks interact than on single regions [5]. Group differences in brain structure are also very small and largely confined to childhood, as [Does ADHD mean my brain is damaged or "three years behind"?](brain-structure-and-development.md) explains.

**Shared genes blur the edges.** An estimated 84–98% of the genetic variants that influence ADHD also influence other conditions, such as autism, depression and anxiety [6]. So the same genetic background can show up as different mixtures of traits, as [Is ADHD genetic, and will my children have it?](genes-and-inheritance.md) explains.

**Some features are common but not specific to ADHD.** One of the most consistent lab findings is that people with ADHD respond with more variable reaction times. A meta-analysis of 319 studies found this, but also that children with ADHD were only slightly more variable than children with *other* clinical conditions (*g* = 0.25 [7]; about 90% overlap by this page's calculation). A feature can be typical of ADHD without being unique to it.

## What is uncertain or debated

- **Whether ADHD should be split into biological subtypes.** Researchers have tried to define subgroups by brain scans, test profiles or genetics. None has yet produced subtypes that reliably predict course or treatment response.
- **How much heterogeneity is in the person and how much is in the measurement.** Lab tests and questionnaires often disagree, and some variation reflects which test was used. More is in [executive function and working memory](../03-why-i-think-and-feel-this-way/executive-function-and-working-memory.md).
- **Most of this evidence comes from children.** Adult subgroup research is thinner.

## What this means for you

If your ADHD does not look like a friend's, or like the descriptions you have read online, that fits the evidence rather than counting against your diagnosis. It also means no single explanation (dopamine, a smaller region, a working-memory deficit) is likely to describe you completely. A normal result on one test does not rule ADHD out, and an unusual result on one test does not rule it in.
