# Why is my ADHD so different from someone else's?

> **Short answer:** Because ADHD is not one fault in one place. Many different combinations of genes, brain development and thinking styles can lead to the same pattern of symptoms. No single brain difference or test weakness is found in everyone with ADHD, and many people with ADHD score normally on lab tests. Two people can share a diagnosis and have quite different underlying profiles.

## What the evidence shows

**The diagnosis itself allows many combinations.** ADHD is diagnosed from lists of inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive symptoms. A person needs a set number from one or both lists, so two people can meet criteria while sharing few symptoms. Clinicians describe the result as predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, or combined presentation, and a person's presentation can change over time. <!-- CHECK: DSM-5-TR presentation names and the "can change over time" wording are from memory. --> See [how diagnosis works](../01-is-this-real/how-diagnosis-works.md).

**Executive weaknesses are common but not universal.** A meta-analysis of 83 studies, covering about 3,700 people with ADHD and 3,000 without, found average weaknesses in executive functions (the mental skills used to plan, hold information in mind and stop an action) [1]. The differences were medium-sized (*d* = 0.46 to 0.69: the two groups' scores overlap by roughly 73–82%). <!-- CHECK: overlap computed by the writer as OVL = 2Φ(−d/2): d=0.46 → 81.8%, d=0.69 → 73.0%. --> The authors concluded that executive weaknesses are "neither necessary nor sufficient" to cause all ADHD: many people with ADHD do not have them on tests [1]. Later reviews put the share of children with ADHD who show executive impairment at roughly a third to a half [2]. <!-- CHECK: the 33–50% figure is attributed to Nigg et al. 2005 (and Biederman 2004) by a secondary review; Nigg 2005 abstract not read. -->

**More than one route can lead to ADHD.** The "dual pathway" model proposes at least two partly separate routes: one through executive difficulties, and one through motivation, especially a strong dislike of waiting (delay aversion) linked to the brain's reward systems [3]. Later work has suggested a third route through timing difficulties. These are models, supported by studies showing the problems are partly separable, not settled facts [3]. See [time, waiting and rewards](../03-why-i-think-and-feel-this-way/time-waiting-and-rewards.md).

**ADHD's variety sits inside ordinary human variety.** A study of 498 children with and without ADHD sorted them by their profiles on thinking tasks [4]. Children *without* ADHD also fell into distinct subgroups, and the subgroups found in ADHD largely mirrored them. The authors described ADHD heterogeneity as "nested" within normal variation [4]. Put plainly, the ways people with ADHD differ from each other are much like the ways everyone differs.

**Brain-imaging studies do not converge on one region.** Across 96 imaging studies in young people, no brain region showed consistent differences, and the authors pointed to heterogeneity as the likely reason [5]. Group differences in brain structure are also very small and largely confined to childhood (see [brain structure and development](brain-structure-and-development.md)).

**Shared genes blur the edges.** An estimated 84–98% of the genetic variants that influence ADHD also influence other conditions, such as autism, depression and anxiety [6]. So the same genetic background can show up as different mixtures of traits. See [genes and inheritance](genes-and-inheritance.md).

**Some features are common but not specific to ADHD.** One of the most consistent lab findings is that people with ADHD respond with more variable reaction times. A meta-analysis of 319 studies found this, but also that children with ADHD were only slightly more variable than children with *other* clinical conditions (*g* = 0.25, an overlap of about 90%) [7]. <!-- CHECK: overlap computed by writer from g=0.25 (OVL=2Φ(−0.125) ≈ 90%). --> A feature can be typical of ADHD without being unique to it.

## What is uncertain or debated

- **Whether ADHD should be split into biological subtypes.** Researchers have tried to define subgroups by brain scans, test profiles or genetics. None has yet produced subtypes that reliably predict course or treatment response.
- **How much heterogeneity is in the person and how much is in the measurement.** Lab tests and questionnaires often disagree (see [executive function and working memory](../03-why-i-think-and-feel-this-way/executive-function-and-working-memory.md)), and some variation reflects which test was used.
- **Most of this evidence comes from children.** Adult subgroup research is thinner.

## What this means for you

If your ADHD does not look like a friend's, or like the descriptions you have read online, that fits the evidence rather than counting against your diagnosis. It also means no single explanation (dopamine, a smaller region, a working-memory deficit) is likely to describe you completely. A normal result on one test does not rule ADHD out, and an unusual result on one test does not rule it in.
