# Where does the information on these pages come from?

> **Short answer:** The corpus leans on international consensus statements, national clinical guidelines, systematic reviews and meta-analyses, and very large registry and genetic studies, in roughly that order of preference. The anchor is the 2021 World Federation of ADHD consensus statement. Every citation was checked by web search against a published record, but the full text of most papers could not be opened while drafting, so some details are flagged for further checking.

## The guidelines and consensus statements

Clinical guidelines tell clinicians what to do; consensus statements summarise what experts agree the evidence shows. Neither is written for patients, but both are the best single place to see where the field stands. They mostly agree on the basics and differ on details such as which medicine to offer first and how to organise services.

### International

- **World Federation of ADHD International Consensus Statement (2021)** [1]. 208 evidence-based conclusions about ADHD, written by 80 authors from 27 countries. A finding was included only if it came from a meta-analysis (of at least five studies or 2,000 people) or from a study of more than 2,000 people. This is the corpus's anchor document for "what is established".
- **European consensus on adult ADHD (Kooij et al., 2019)** [2]. An updated European expert consensus on diagnosing and treating adults. It describes adult ADHD as often lifelong and underdiagnosed in Europe.
- **International consensus on girls and women (Young et al., 2020)** [3]. An expert consensus on why girls and women are under-recognised and how assessment should adapt. It is a consensus, not a systematic review.

### National

- **United Kingdom: NICE guideline NG87** [4]. *Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder: diagnosis and management.* Published March 2018, last substantially updated September 2019. Minor edits followed in January 2023 (describing ADHD as a neurodevelopmental disorder and moving to ICD-11 terms) and May 2025 (a link to NICE guidance on digital assessment tools). NICE's page records that it was last reviewed in July 2026. Covers children, young people and adults.
- **Australia: AADPA guideline** [5]. *Australian Evidence-Based Clinical Practice Guideline for ADHD*, from the Australian ADHD Professionals Association (now the Australasian ADHD Professionals Association). Its recommendations were approved by the National Health and Medical Research Council on 29 July 2022; it was published in October 2022 and summarised in a journal paper in 2023. Official sources disagree on how many recommendations it contains: the journal summary says 113, while AADPA's own product page and the Australian Government's announcement say 111. AADPA has since posted revised versions of the guideline document, the latest dated January 2026. Covers all ages.
- **Canada: CADDRA Canadian ADHD Practice Guidelines** [6]. The current edition is 4.1 (dated 2020; the published document is labelled January 2021). In July 2026 CADDRA board members previewed upcoming guideline updates at the Canadian Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry conference, but no release date had been announced when this page was written (November 2026).
- **United States: APSARD adult guideline (not yet published)** [7]. The American Professional Society of ADHD and Related Disorders is writing the first US guideline for diagnosing and treating ADHD in adults. It entered its final review stage in August 2026 and publication was expected in autumn 2026, though one report suggested 2027. Searches on 24 November 2026 found no published version. Check apsard.org for its current status. US guidance for children comes from the American Academy of Pediatrics, which this corpus does not rely on. <!-- CHECK: APSARD status confirmed unpublished on 24 November 2026; re-search before release -->

## The other source types the corpus uses

- **Umbrella reviews and network meta-analyses** for treatment. A network meta-analysis compares many treatments at once, including ones never tested head to head. An umbrella review pools many meta-analyses. The 2025 *BMJ* umbrella review by Gosling and colleagues [8] covers 221 meta-analyses of 31 treatments and links to a public decision-support database (ebiadhd-database.org).
- **Cochrane reviews.** Cochrane is an international network known for strict appraisal of trial quality. Its reviews sometimes rate evidence lower than other groups do; the corpus reports both where they disagree, for example on methylphenidate in children.
- **National registry studies**, mainly from Sweden and Denmark, which link health, prescription and other records for whole populations. They give large numbers and allow within-person and sibling designs, but they only include diagnosed people and cannot prove cause. See [how to read the evidence](how-to-read-the-evidence.md).
- **Large genetic and imaging consortia** such as the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium and ENIGMA, which pool data from many sites.

Single small studies appear only where nothing better exists, and are labelled **Limited**.

## How sources were chosen

1. **Prefer the highest level available.** Consensus statements and guidelines first, then systematic reviews and meta-analyses, then large registry or genetic studies, then individual trials and cohorts.
2. **Prefer recent where it matters.** Treatment evidence moves faster than genetics, so treatment pages favour 2018–2025 reviews.
3. **Report disagreement.** Where good sources conflict, pages give both positions rather than picking one.
4. **One home per fact.** Each key number is given in full on one page and linked from elsewhere, so the corpus does not carry several versions of the same figure.
5. **Note funding where relevant.** Some registry studies were funded by drug manufacturers. This is stated where it applies, without implying the findings are wrong.

## What the network limits meant for checking

The corpus was drafted in an environment that could run web searches but could not open PubMed, doi.org, Crossref or Europe PMC directly. In practice:

- Every cited paper was confirmed to exist through search results showing its bibliographic record (authors, title, journal, year, and usually a DOI or PubMed ID). No DOI was constructed or guessed.
- Many numbers come from abstracts shown in those results. Full texts, tables and supplementary material were mostly not read.
- Where a detail came from memory, from a secondary report, or from an identifier that could not be confirmed, the page source carries a hidden checking note for reviewers. These are being worked through.

So the corpus is better than unsourced writing and short of a professionally fact-checked review. If you are relying on a specific number, open the cited source. If you find a mismatch, please report it ([CONTRIBUTING](../CONTRIBUTING.md)).

## What is uncertain or debated

Guidelines lag behind research by several years, and NICE, AADPA and CADDRA were all written before the 2025 treatment reviews. Guidelines also reflect local health systems, so a recommendation that suits the NHS may not transfer elsewhere. Consensus statements can carry the views of the experts who wrote them. Several authors of the World Federation of ADHD statement, for example, declared financial links with drug companies, which the statement discloses.

## What this means for you

If you want to know what your clinician is likely working from, the guideline for your country is the best place to look. NICE publishes information for the public alongside NG87, and AADPA publishes a consumer companion to its guideline. This corpus is a guide to the research behind those guidelines, not a substitute for them.
