# Where does the information on these pages come from?

> **Short answer:** These pages lean mainly on consensus statements and national clinical guidelines, then systematic reviews, meta-analyses and very large registry and genetic studies. The anchor is the World Federation of ADHD consensus statement (2021, updated 2026). Claims were checked against abstracts and PubMed or Crossref records, rarely full texts, and no clinician has reviewed the pages.

## How these pages were written

- **Who maintains them.** The pages are maintained by the owner of this GitHub repository, who is not a clinician.
- **How they were drafted.** They were drafted with AI assistance. A first set of AI writers drafted each page from published sources, chosen as described further down this page.
- **How they were checked.** Separate AI checkers then tested the claims against the cited sources, in two waves. Figures that could not be confirmed were either removed or kept with their origin, such as a press release or a secondary summary, stated in the text.
- **What the checking rested on.** In the first two waves, PubMed, doi.org, Crossref and Europe PMC could not be opened directly, so confirmation rested on abstracts and index records shown in web search results. In a third check (November 2026), most sources were matched against their PubMed or Crossref record and the numbers against the abstract; a few were checked against open-access full texts. Tables and supplementary material were still rarely read.
- **Who has not reviewed them.** No clinician has reviewed these pages.

In practice, each cited paper was looked for in search results showing its bibliographic record (authors, title, journal, year, and usually a DOI or PubMed ID). Where part of a record could not be confirmed, it was removed or replaced with a short description in square brackets, and no DOI was constructed or guessed. Where a detail rested on memory, a secondary report or an unconfirmed identifier, checkers flagged it, and each flag was resolved by removing the detail or saying on the page where it came from.

So these pages are better than unsourced writing and short of a professionally fact-checked review. If you are relying on a specific number, open the cited source.

Corrections go through GitHub issues. If you find a mismatch or an error, please open an issue as the [contributing guide](../.github/CONTRIBUTING.md) describes.

## The guidelines and consensus statements

Clinical guidelines tell clinicians what to do; consensus statements summarise what experts agree the evidence shows. Neither is written for patients, but both are the best single place to see where the field stands. They mostly agree on the basics and differ on details such as which medicine to offer first and how to organise services.

### International

- **World Federation of ADHD International Consensus Statement (2021)** [1]. 208 evidence-based conclusions about ADHD, written by about 80 authors from 27 countries (published records give 79 or 80). A finding was included only if it came from a meta-analysis (of at least five studies or 2,000 people) or from a study of more than 2,000 people. This is the anchor document these pages use for "what is established".
- **World Federation of ADHD International Consensus Statement: 2026 update** [2]. Published online in November 2026, it adds 252 statements drawn from studies published between January 2021 and July 2025, using the same rules (meta-analyses of at least five studies or 2,000 people, with publication bias considered, or studies of 2,000 or more people). It was approved by 118 authors from 29 countries. Its authors say most of the 2021 conclusions still stand, with added nuance and a few areas where the balance of findings has shifted. These pages were written before it appeared and have not yet been checked against each of its statements.
- **European consensus on adult ADHD (Kooij et al., 2019)** [3]. An updated European expert consensus on diagnosing and treating adults. It describes adult ADHD as often lifelong and underdiagnosed in Europe.
- **International consensus on girls and women (Young et al., 2020)** [4]. An expert consensus on why girls and women are under-recognised and how assessment should adapt. It is a consensus, not a systematic review.

### National

- **United Kingdom: NICE guideline NG87** [5]. *Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder: diagnosis and management.* Published March 2018, last substantially updated September 2019. Minor edits followed in January 2023 (describing ADHD as a neurodevelopmental disorder and moving to ICD-11 terms) and May 2025 (a link to NICE guidance on digital assessment tools). NICE's page records that it was last reviewed in July 2026. Covers children, young people and adults.
- **Australia: AADPA guideline** [6]. *Australian Evidence-Based Clinical Practice Guideline for ADHD*, from the Australian ADHD Professionals Association (now the Australasian ADHD Professionals Association). Its recommendations were approved by the National Health and Medical Research Council on 29 July 2022; it was published in October 2022 and summarised in a journal paper in 2023. Official sources disagree on how many recommendations it contains: the journal summary says 113, while AADPA's own product page and the Australian Government's announcement say 111. AADPA has since posted revised versions of the guideline document, the latest dated January 2026. Covers all ages.
- **Canada: CADDRA Canadian ADHD Practice Guidelines** [7]. The current edition is 4.1 (dated 2020; the published document is labelled January 2021). In July 2026 CADDRA board members previewed upcoming guideline updates at the Canadian Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry conference, but no release date had been announced when this page was written (November 2026).
- **United States: APSARD adult guideline (not yet published)** [8]. The American Professional Society of ADHD and Related Disorders is writing the first US guideline for diagnosing and treating ADHD in adults. It entered its final review stage in August 2026 and publication was expected in autumn 2026, though one report suggested 2027. Searches on 24 November 2026 found no published version, so check apsard.org for its current status. US guidance for children comes from the American Academy of Pediatrics, which these pages do not rely on.

## The other source types these pages use

- **Umbrella reviews and network meta-analyses** for treatment. A network meta-analysis compares many treatments at once, including ones never tested head to head. An umbrella review pools many meta-analyses. The 2025 *BMJ* umbrella review by Gosling and colleagues [9] covers 221 treatment–comparison–outcome combinations across 31 treatments and links to a public decision-support database (ebiadhd-database.org).
- **Cochrane reviews.** Cochrane is an international network known for strict appraisal of trial quality. Its reviews sometimes rate evidence lower than other groups do; these pages report both where they disagree, for example on methylphenidate in children.
- **National registry studies**, mainly from Sweden and Denmark, which link health, prescription and other records for whole populations. They give large numbers and allow within-person and sibling designs, but they only include diagnosed people and cannot prove cause. These designs are explained in [Reading ADHD research: what do the numbers really mean?](how-to-read-the-evidence.md)
- **Large genetic and imaging consortia** such as the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium and ENIGMA, which pool data from many sites.

Single small studies appear only where nothing better exists, and are labelled **Limited**.

To see the whole source base in charts (how strong the evidence is in each folder, what kinds of study it is, and how recent), go to [What evidence do these pages rest on, taken as a whole?](the-evidence-at-a-glance.md)

## How sources were chosen

1. **Prefer the highest level available.** Consensus statements and guidelines first, then systematic reviews and meta-analyses, then large registry or genetic studies, then individual trials and cohorts.
2. **Prefer recent where it matters.** Treatment evidence moves faster than genetics, so treatment pages favour 2018–2025 reviews.
3. **Report disagreement.** Where good sources conflict, pages give both positions rather than picking one.
4. **One home per fact.** Each key number is given in full, with its caveats, on one home page. Other pages may give the headline figure, matching the home page, and link to it for the detail.
5. **Note funding where it was seen.** Some registry studies were funded by drug manufacturers. This is noted where it was seen, without implying the findings are wrong; funding was not reviewed study by study for every page.

## What is uncertain or debated

Guidelines lag behind research by several years, and NICE, AADPA and CADDRA were all written before the 2025 treatment reviews. Guidelines also reflect local health systems, so a recommendation that suits the NHS may not transfer elsewhere.

Consensus statements can carry the views of the experts who wrote them. Several authors of the World Federation of ADHD statement, for example, declared financial links with drug companies, which the statement discloses.

## What this means for you

If you want to know what your clinician is likely working from, the guideline for your country is the best place to look. NICE publishes information for the public alongside NG87, and AADPA publishes a consumer companion to its guideline. These pages are a guide to the research behind those guidelines, not a substitute for them.
