# How do I make sense of the numbers on these pages?

> **Short answer:** Most ADHD findings describe averages across large groups, and the groups overlap far more than headlines suggest. A "higher risk" means little until you know the starting risk, and a link between two things is not proof that one causes the other. This page explains the handful of ideas that the rest of the corpus relies on, using real ADHD numbers, and what the Strong / Moderate / Limited / Contested labels mean.

## The ideas, with worked examples

### Effect sizes and how much groups overlap

An effect size (often written *d*, *g* or SMD, the standardised mean difference) measures how far apart two group averages are, in units of how spread out people are. By convention 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. The most useful way to picture one is overlap: how much the two groups' scores share the same range.

- Brain-region volumes in ADHD differ from controls by about *d* = 0.1 to 0.2 [1]. At *d* = 0.2 the two groups overlap by about 92%, so most people with ADHD have measurements well inside the ordinary range. This is why no scan can diagnose ADHD in one person. <!-- CHECK: overlap figures are the scoping report's own calculation (OVL = 2Φ(−d/2)), not from a paper -->
- Executive function test differences are moderate, around *d* = 0.46 to 0.69 [2]. At *d* = 0.5 the groups still overlap by about 80%, which fits the finding that many people with ADHD score normally on these tests.
- Medication trials in children report effects of roughly 0.7 to 0.8 on symptom ratings. At 0.8, overlap is still about 69%: a large average effect, yet not everyone improves and the size of benefit varies a lot. The full figures are on [how well medication works](../06-treatment-what-works/how-well-medication-works.md).

### Heritability: what 74% does and does not mean

Twin studies put the heritability of ADHD at about 74% on average across 37 studies [3]. Heritability is the share of the *differences between people in a population* that is linked to genetic differences between them.

It does **not** mean that 74% of one person's ADHD comes from genes, and it does not mean ADHD is fixed or unaffected by surroundings. It also depends on the population and the measure: in Swedish twins, heritability of clinically diagnosed ADHD was 0.88, and 0.72 in adults [4]. Studies that read DNA directly (genome-wide association studies, or GWAS) capture a smaller share, about 22% [5], because thousands of variants each have tiny effects. For family risk and what genetics can and cannot predict, see [genes and inheritance](../02-whats-happening-in-my-brain/genes-and-inheritance.md).

### Relative risk versus absolute risk

A relative risk says how many times more common something is in one group. An absolute risk says how common it actually is. Both are needed.

- In Danish registry data, people with ADHD died at a rate of 5.85 per 10,000 person-years against 2.21 for people without ADHD [6]. Per year, that is about 6 in 10,000 against about 2 in 10,000. The fully adjusted rate was about twice as high (a mortality rate ratio of 2.07). "Twice as likely to die" is accurate and frightening; "about four extra deaths per 10,000 people per year, mostly from accidents" is equally accurate and lets you judge it. Context is on [the hard statistics in context](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/the-hard-statistics-in-context.md).
- In US insurance data, children born in August, the youngest in their school year, were diagnosed with ADHD at 85.1 per 10,000 against 63.6 per 10,000 for September-born children [7]. That is about 34% higher in relative terms, and 21.5 extra diagnoses per 10,000 children in absolute terms.

A "person-year" is one person followed for one year, so 10,000 person-years could be 10,000 people for a year or 1,000 people for ten.

### Association versus causation, and sibling designs

An association means two things tend to occur together. It can arise because one causes the other, or because something else (a confounder) drives both. Because ADHD is strongly inherited, families share both genes and circumstances, which makes confounding common.

Sibling comparison studies compare brothers and sisters who differ in an exposure. They share much of their genes and home, so if the link disappears between siblings, the original link was probably about the family rather than the exposure.

- Smoking in pregnancy was linked to ADHD in children with a hazard ratio (a measure of relative risk over time) of about 1.9 to 2.5. Comparing siblings, the link vanished (about 0.84 to 0.88) [8].
- Paracetamol in pregnancy showed a small link across 2.48 million Swedish children (1.07), which disappeared in full-sibling comparisons (0.98) [9].

### Within-person designs

Within-person designs compare the same people with themselves during periods on and off medication. This removes stable differences between people, such as personality or family background. In Swedish data, criminal convictions were 32% lower in men and 41% lower in women during medicated periods [10]. These designs are strong, but still observational: something else that changes at the same time as treatment (a new job, a crisis) can still explain part of the result. See [what registries show](../06-treatment-what-works/what-registries-show.md).

### Blinded versus unblinded ratings

A rating is blinded when the person scoring symptoms does not know who received the treatment. Parents who know their child is on a new diet tend to rate improvement. A review of child trials found that diets and psychological treatments all looked effective on unblinded ratings; with probably-blinded raters, only fatty acid supplements (SMD 0.16, a small effect) and food-colour exclusion in selected children stayed significant [11]. Neurofeedback showed essentially no effect on blinded ratings (SMD 0.04) [12]. Whenever a page reports a non-drug treatment, check whether the raters were blinded.

## The evidence labels used on every page

Each source on every page carries one label:

| Label | What it means here |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | Large, well-designed studies or meta-analyses that agree. Unlikely to be overturned. |
| **Moderate** | Consistent findings, but fewer studies, observational designs or known limits. Could shift. |
| **Limited** | Small, few or self-report studies. Treat as a lead, not a conclusion. |
| **Contested** | Good-quality sources disagree, or experts dispute how to read the same data. |

A meta-analysis pools many studies; a systematic review searches for all relevant studies by a stated method. Neither guarantees quality: a meta-analysis of small, unblinded trials is still small and unblinded.

## What is uncertain or debated

Effect-size conventions are rough guides, and "small" effects can matter when they apply to many people or to serious outcomes. Overlap figures assume bell-shaped distributions, which real data only approximate. Sibling designs have their own weak spots: siblings differ in ways the study cannot see, and errors in recording an exposure can push a sibling estimate towards zero, making a real effect look absent. Groups that grade evidence also differ in how strictly they judge bias; the methylphenidate disagreement between Cochrane and the 2025 BMJ umbrella review is about grading, not about the size of the effect.

## What this means for you

No group statistic on these pages can tell you about your own brain, genes or future. When you meet a frightening number, ask three questions: what was the starting risk, who was studied, and could something else explain the link? The pages are written so those answers sit next to the number.
