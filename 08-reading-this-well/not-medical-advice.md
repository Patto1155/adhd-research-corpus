# Can these pages tell me what to do about my ADHD?

> [!IMPORTANT]
> If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, help is open now. Phone and text services for Australia, the UK, Ireland, the US, Canada and New Zealand are listed under [If you need help now](#if-you-need-help-now), just below.

> **Short answer:** No. These pages explain what research has found about ADHD across groups of people. They cannot diagnose you, say whether a treatment suits you, or tell you to start, stop or change any medicine; those decisions belong with a clinician who knows your history.

## If you need help now

If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number.

### Australia

- Emergency: 000
- **Lifeline 13 11 14** (call), text 0477 13 11 14, or chat at lifeline.org.au. 24 hours.

### United Kingdom

- Emergency: 999, or go to A&E
- **Samaritans 116 123**, free, 24 hours.
- In England, **NHS 111** and choose the mental health option for urgent mental health help.

### Ireland

- Emergency: 112 or 999
- **Samaritans 116 123**, free, 24 hours.
- **Pieta 1800 247 247**, or text HELP to 51444 (standard text rates).

### United States

- Emergency: 911
- **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline**: call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org.

### Canada

- Emergency: 911
- **9-8-8 Suicide Crisis Helpline**: call or text 9-8-8, 24 hours, in English and French.

### New Zealand

- Emergency: 111
- **1737 Need to talk?**: free call or text 1737, 24 hours.

### Anywhere else

[findahelpline.com](https://findahelpline.com) lists free, confidential helplines in more than 130 countries, searchable by country and topic. It is a directory, not an emergency service, so in immediate danger call your local emergency number first.

These numbers were checked by web search in November 2026. Services change; if a number does not connect, use findahelpline.com or your local emergency number.

## What these pages can and cannot do

**They can** summarise what large studies, reviews and clinical guidelines have found; show how strong that evidence is; say what is unknown; and give you clearer questions to take to a clinician.

**They cannot:**

- **Diagnose.** ADHD is diagnosed through a clinical assessment of symptoms, history across settings, and impairment, after other explanations have been considered. Online quizzes and screening questionnaires flag people for assessment; they do not diagnose. More is on [how diagnosis works](../01-is-this-real/how-diagnosis-works.md).
- **Tell you what is safe for you.** Studies report average risks in groups. Your own risk depends on your health, history and other medicines, which only someone assessing you can weigh.
- **Advise on medication.** Starting, stopping, changing dose or switching medicine are decisions to make with your prescriber. Where pages report a dose that a study found mattered, that is the study's finding, not a recommendation.
- **Replace local information.** Rules on driving, work, insurance, prescribing and access to assessment differ between countries and states, and fall outside what these pages cover.
- **Recommend products.** Pages report the evidence on supplements, diets, apps and devices. They do not tell you what to buy.

Most of the evidence on these pages comes from children, from people with a diagnosis, or from particular countries (often Scandinavia, the UK and the US). Each page says when that affects how far a finding applies.

## When to talk to a clinician

Talk to your GP, family doctor or prescriber if:

- you think you may have ADHD and it is affecting your work, study, relationships or safety;
- you have a diagnosis and something has changed, such as mood, sleep, appetite, heart symptoms (palpitations, chest pain, fainting) or new unusual experiences such as hearing or seeing things others do not;
- you are thinking about changing or stopping a medicine, or are pregnant, planning a pregnancy or breastfeeding;
- you are using alcohol or other drugs in a way that worries you;
- something you read here, or anywhere else, has made you anxious about your treatment.

Seek help urgently, using the contacts above, if you are thinking about suicide or self-harm, or are worried someone else is.

## A note on suicide and hard statistics

Some pages discuss suicide, self-harm and mortality, because readers ask about them and because accurate numbers are less frightening than rumours. They follow safe-messaging practice: no detail about methods, absolute numbers alongside relative ones wherever they were found, and a link back here.

If reading them is hard, it is fine to stop and come back later, or to read them with someone.

## Who wrote this and how

These pages were drafted with AI assistance, and separate AI checkers then tested the claims against the abstracts and PubMed or Crossref records of the cited sources, in three rounds, rarely against their full texts. They are not written by or for a clinical service, and no clinician has reviewed them. Errors can remain; the full account is in [where the information on these pages comes from](sources-and-guidelines.md#how-these-pages-were-written).

If you find an error, please open an issue on GitHub, as the [contributing guide](../.github/CONTRIBUTING.md) describes.
