# What do the technical terms on these pages mean?

> **Short answer:** This is an alphabetical list of the terms you will meet across the corpus, each explained in one to three sentences. Pages define a term the first time they use it; this list is for when you have forgotten or want to check. For the ideas behind effect sizes, heritability and risk, see [how to read the evidence](how-to-read-the-evidence.md), which explains them with worked examples.

## A

**Absolute risk.** How often something actually happens in a group, for example 6 in 10,000 people per year. Always read it alongside relative risk.

**Actigraphy.** Measuring sleep and activity with a wrist-worn movement sensor over days or weeks. It is more objective than a sleep diary but less detailed than a sleep-lab study.

**ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder).** A neurodevelopmental condition defined by persistent inattention and/or hyperactivity and impulsivity that start in childhood and cause problems in daily life. "ADD" is an older name, now covered by the inattentive presentation.

**ADHD-RS (ADHD Rating Scale).** A standard questionnaire scoring the 18 ADHD symptoms, used in many medication trials to measure change.

**Adjusted.** A result is adjusted when the analysis has statistically accounted for other factors, such as age or sex, that could explain the link. Adjustment only covers the factors that were measured.

**Alpha-2A agonists (α2A agonists).** Non-stimulant ADHD medicines, guanfacine and clonidine, that act on noradrenaline receptors in the prefrontal cortex.

**Amphetamines.** A family of stimulant medicines (including dexamfetamine and lisdexamfetamine) that block the reuptake of dopamine and noradrenaline and also release them from nerve cells.

**ASRS (Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale).** A short questionnaire used to screen adults for possible ADHD. A high score suggests an assessment is worthwhile; it is not a diagnosis.

**Association.** Two things occurring together more often than chance would predict. An association does not show that one causes the other.

**Atomoxetine.** A non-stimulant medicine that blocks reuptake of noradrenaline. It usually takes several weeks to reach full effect.

**Autism (autism spectrum disorder).** A neurodevelopmental condition involving differences in social communication and repetitive behaviours or interests. It often co-occurs with ADHD.

## B

**Blinding.** Keeping participants, clinicians or raters unaware of who received which treatment, so expectations cannot sway the results. A "probably blinded" rating is one made by someone unlikely to know, such as a teacher not told about a trial.

## C

**Case-control study.** A study that starts with people who have an outcome (cases) and compares their past exposures with people who do not (controls). Useful for rare outcomes, but prone to bias in how cases and controls are chosen.

**Catecholamines.** The chemical family that includes dopamine and noradrenaline, the main messengers that ADHD medicines act on.

**Certainty of evidence.** A rating of how confident reviewers are that a result is close to the truth, often graded high, moderate, low or very low using a system called GRADE. Two groups can grade the same data differently.

**Chronotype.** A person's natural tendency to sleep and wake earlier or later. People with ADHD are, on average, later chronotypes.

**Circadian rhythm.** The body's roughly 24-hour internal clock, which controls sleep timing, hormone release and alertness.

**Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT).** A structured talking therapy that works on thoughts, habits and practical skills. Adapted versions for adult ADHD focus on organisation, planning and procrastination.

**Cochrane review.** A systematic review produced under the methods of Cochrane, an international research network known for strict judgements of study quality.

**Cohen's d.** See *effect size*.

**Cohort study.** A study that follows a group of people over time and records what happens to them.

**Co-occurring condition (comorbidity).** A second condition present at the same time, such as anxiety alongside ADHD.

**Confidence interval.** The range within which the true value is likely to lie, given the data. A wide interval means the estimate is imprecise.

**Confounding.** When a third factor drives both an exposure and an outcome, creating a link between them that is not causal. Shared family genes are a common confounder in ADHD research.

**Consensus statement.** A document in which a group of experts sets out what they agree the evidence shows. Its strength depends on the evidence and the process behind it.

**Cortex.** The brain's thin, folded outer layer, involved in thinking, planning and perception. Studies measure its thickness and surface area.

## D

**DAT (dopamine transporter).** A protein that pulls dopamine back into nerve cells after it has been released. Methylphenidate and amphetamines block it.

**Delay discounting.** The tendency to value a reward less the longer you must wait for it. People with ADHD discount delayed rewards more steeply, on average.

**Diagnostic criteria.** The agreed list of symptoms, duration, age of onset and impairment that must be met for a diagnosis, set out in the DSM and ICD.

**Dimensional.** Describes a trait that varies by degree across everyone, rather than being simply present or absent. ADHD traits are dimensional; a diagnosis is a threshold on that dimension.

**DLMO (dim-light melatonin onset).** The time in the evening when the body starts releasing melatonin under dim light. It is the standard laboratory marker of body-clock timing.

**Dopamine.** A brain chemical messenger involved in motivation, reward, movement and attention. It is one of the main targets of ADHD medicines.

**DSM-5-TR.** The *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders*, 5th edition, text revision (2022), published by the American Psychiatric Association. It is used for diagnosis in the US, Australia and many other places.

## E

**Effect size.** A measure of how big a difference is between groups, in units of how spread out people are. Common versions are Cohen's *d*, Hedges' *g* and the standardised mean difference (SMD); roughly, 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large.

**Emotion dysregulation.** Difficulty controlling the strength or duration of emotional reactions. It is common in ADHD but is not part of the diagnostic criteria.

**ENIGMA.** An international consortium that pools brain scans from many research sites to get large samples. Its ADHD working group produced the main studies on brain volume.

**Executive function.** The mental skills used to plan, start, organise, switch between and stop tasks, including working memory and inhibition.

## F

**Familial confounding.** Confounding caused by factors shared within families, such as genes or home environment. Sibling comparisons are designed to remove much of it.

## G

**Genome-wide association study (GWAS).** A study that scans hundreds of thousands of genetic markers across many people to find variants more common in those with a condition. Each variant found usually has a very small effect.

**Guideline (clinical guideline).** Recommendations for clinicians, produced by a national or professional body after reviewing the evidence, such as NICE NG87 in the UK.

## H

**Hazard ratio (HR).** A relative measure of how fast an event happens in one group compared with another over time. An HR of 2 means the event happens at about twice the rate; it does not tell you how common the event is.

**Heritability.** The share of differences between people in a particular population that is linked to genetic differences between them. It does not describe one person, and it does not mean fixed or unchangeable.

**Heterogeneity.** Variation, either between people with the same diagnosis or between the results of different studies. High heterogeneity in a meta-analysis means studies disagree more than chance would explain.

**Hyperfocus.** Periods of intense, hard-to-break absorption in an activity. Many people with ADHD report it, but it has been little studied and is not a diagnostic feature.

## I

**ICD-11.** The *International Classification of Diseases*, 11th revision, from the World Health Organization. It is used for diagnosis and health records in the UK, Europe and much of the world.

**Impairment.** Real-world difficulty caused by symptoms, at work, study, home, in relationships or in safety. A diagnosis of ADHD requires it.

**Inverted U.** A relationship where moderate levels of something work best and too little or too much make things worse. Prefrontal function and catecholamine levels follow this pattern in animal studies.

## M

**Masking (camouflaging).** Hiding or compensating for difficulties in order to fit in. It is one reason ADHD can be missed, especially in girls and women.

**Mega-analysis.** Pooling the raw data from many studies and analysing it together, rather than combining their published results.

**Meta-analysis.** A statistical method that combines the results of many studies on the same question into one overall estimate.

**Methylphenidate.** A stimulant medicine that blocks reuptake of dopamine and noradrenaline. It is sold under brand names such as Ritalin and Concerta.

**Minimal clinically important difference.** The smallest change on a scale that patients or clinicians would notice as meaningful.

**Mortality rate ratio (MRR).** The death rate in one group divided by the death rate in another. Read it alongside the actual death rates.

## N

**Network meta-analysis.** A meta-analysis that compares several treatments at once, including pairs never tested against each other directly.

**Neurodevelopmental.** Describes conditions, such as ADHD and autism, that arise from differences in how the brain develops, usually becoming visible in childhood.

**Neurodiversity.** The view that differences in how brains work are part of natural human variation, rather than only disorders. It can sit alongside a clinical view.

**Neurofeedback.** A training method in which people watch a live display of their brain activity and try to change it. Blinded trials have not shown it reduces ADHD symptoms.

**Noradrenaline (norepinephrine).** A brain chemical messenger involved in alertness and attention. Many ADHD medicines act on it.

**Non-stimulant.** An ADHD medicine that is not a stimulant, such as atomoxetine, viloxazine, guanfacine or clonidine.

## O

**Observational study.** A study that records what happens without assigning treatment. It can show associations but not prove cause.

**Odds ratio (OR).** A relative measure comparing the odds of an outcome in two groups. When outcomes are rare it is close to the relative risk.

**Overlap.** How much two groups' scores fall in the same range. Small effect sizes mean large overlap.

## P

**Person-years.** One person followed for one year. Rates per person-year allow fair comparisons when people are followed for different lengths of time.

**PET and SPECT.** Brain-scanning methods that use small amounts of a radioactive tracer to measure chemistry, such as how much dopamine transporter is present. They are research tools, not diagnostic tests for ADHD.

**Placebo.** An inactive treatment that looks the same as the real one, used so that expectation effects can be measured.

**Polygenic.** Influenced by many genes, each with a tiny effect. ADHD is polygenic.

**Polygenic score.** A number summarising how many common risk variants a person carries. In ADHD it differs between groups on average but cannot diagnose or predict for an individual.

**Prefrontal cortex.** The front part of the cortex, important for planning, attention and self-control.

**Presentation.** The pattern of ADHD symptoms at a given time: mainly inattentive, mainly hyperactive-impulsive, or combined. Presentations can change over life.

**Prevalence.** The share of a population with a condition at a given time.

**Psychoeducation.** Structured information about a condition and how to manage it, given to patients or families.

## R

**Randomised controlled trial (RCT).** A study that assigns people by chance to a treatment or a comparison, so that the groups differ only by the treatment. It is the strongest design for testing whether a treatment works.

**Registry study.** A study using national records, such as Swedish or Danish health and prescription registers, that cover whole populations.

**Rejection sensitivity.** A tendency to expect, notice and react strongly to rejection. It is studied in psychology across many conditions.

**Rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD).** A term from clinical practice for intense emotional pain after perceived rejection. It is not a recognised diagnosis and has little research behind it.

**Relative age effect.** The finding that the youngest children in a school year are diagnosed with ADHD more often than the oldest.

**Relative risk.** How many times more (or less) likely an outcome is in one group than another. Always read it alongside absolute risk.

**Remission and persistence.** Remission means no longer meeting diagnostic criteria; persistence means continuing to meet them. Partial remission means fewer symptoms but continued difficulty.

## S

**Screening tool.** A short questionnaire that identifies who might have a condition and should be assessed. It does not diagnose.

**Sibling comparison.** A study design comparing siblings who differ in an exposure, which removes much of the confounding from shared genes and family environment.

**SNP heritability.** The share of differences between people that can be explained by common genetic variants measured directly in DNA. It is lower than twin heritability.

**Standardised mean difference (SMD).** See *effect size*.

**Statistically significant.** Unlikely to be due to chance alone, by a conventional threshold. It says nothing about whether a difference is large or matters.

**Stimulants.** The main group of ADHD medicines, methylphenidate and amphetamines, which increase dopamine and noradrenaline signalling.

**Striatum.** A group of structures deep in the brain, including the caudate, putamen and nucleus accumbens, involved in reward and movement.

**Subcortical.** Below the cortex. Subcortical structures include the striatum, amygdala and hippocampus.

**Systematic review.** A review that searches for all relevant studies using a stated, repeatable method, and appraises their quality.

## T

**Target-trial emulation.** A way of analysing observational data so that it mimics, as closely as possible, a randomised trial that was never run.

**Twin study.** A study comparing identical and non-identical twins to estimate how much of the variation in a trait is linked to genes.

## U

**Umbrella review.** A review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses, used to summarise a whole field.

**Unblinded rating.** A rating made by someone who knows which treatment a person received, such as a parent in a diet trial. Unblinded ratings tend to overstate benefits.

## V

**Viloxazine.** A newer non-stimulant medicine that acts mainly on noradrenaline. It has been approved for ADHD in the United States since 2021; no approval for ADHD in other countries was found when this was checked in November 2026.

## W

**Within-person design.** A study comparing the same people with themselves at different times, for example during months on and off medication. It removes stable differences between people but not changes that happen at the same time as treatment.

**Working memory.** The ability to hold and use information in mind for a short time, such as remembering a phone number while dialling it.
