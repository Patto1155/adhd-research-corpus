# Addiction and ADHD: is risk higher, and do medicines raise it?

> **Short answer:** Yes, on average people with ADHD are more likely to smoke and to develop alcohol and other substance problems. Long-term studies find that childhood stimulant treatment neither raises nor lowers later addiction risk, though medicated periods have fewer short-term substance-related problems. Misuse of prescription stimulants, and passing them to others, is a real and separate problem.

## What the evidence shows

**Children with ADHD are more likely to go on to smoke and drink heavily.** A meta-analysis of long-term follow-up studies found that children with ADHD had about 2.4 times the odds of later nicotine use (odds ratio 2.36) by middle adolescence, and about 1.35 times the odds of an alcohol use disorder by young adulthood [1]. These two results held up best in checks; the result for other drugs depended heavily on a single study [1]. The figures come from children followed into adolescence and adulthood.

**In adults, substance use disorders are several times more common.** A 2023 review of large studies (more than 10,000 people each) found adults with ADHD had about 4.6 times the odds of a substance use disorder compared with adults without ADHD [2]. Substance use disorders were more common in men, as they are in the general population [2]. A pooled absolute lifetime risk was not reported in the sources checked for this page, so it is not possible to say here what share of adults with ADHD develop one.

**About one in four people in addiction treatment has ADHD.** Across studies of people being treated for a substance use disorder, about 23% had ADHD [3]. Because ADHD can be hard to assess during heavy substance use or withdrawal, specialist assessment is usually needed in this group.

**Prescribed medication has not been found to raise later addiction risk.** A 2013 meta-analysis of 15 long-term studies found that children treated with stimulants were no more and no less likely to develop substance problems later than those who were not [4]. The longest single follow-up, of 579 children in the US-Canadian Multimodal Treatment Study of ADHD (MTA) followed to an average age of 25, found no link in either direction between current, past or longer stimulant treatment and adult heavy drinking, cannabis, daily smoking or other drug use, after adjusting for factors that change over time [5]. A Swedish registry study found that people prescribed stimulants in 2006 had a 31% lower rate of substance-related hospital visits, crime or death in 2009 [6].

**Within the same person, medicated months had fewer substance-related problems.** In US insurance data on nearly 3 million adolescents and adults with ADHD, the odds of a substance-related event, such as an emergency department visit, were 35% lower for men and 31% lower for women in months on medication than in months off it [7]. Two years later, the reduction was 19% for men and 14% for women [7]. A 2025 Swedish study comparing people who did and did not start medication found a 15% lower rate of substance misuse over two years [8].

Comparing a person with themselves removes fixed differences between people, such as genes and upbringing, though not things that change over time. The authors of [7] warned that this does not show ADHD medication treats substance problems. The design is explained on [Real-life outcomes on ADHD medication: what do registries show?](../06-treatment-what-works/what-registries-show.md)

**Misuse and diversion of prescribed stimulants are real.** A systematic review of 111 studies, mostly of college students, found that between 2% and 59% of people in different samples reported using prescription stimulants not as prescribed [9]. Reported rates of diversion (giving or selling prescribed stimulants to someone else) ranged from under 1% to 80% [9]. The ranges are wide because samples and questions differed.

Most non-medical use was linked to no or minor medical effects, but serious harm, including death, has occurred, especially when tablets were crushed and snorted or injected rather than swallowed [9]. There was little evidence that stimulants improve grades in people without ADHD [9].

## What is uncertain or debated

- **Why the link exists.** Shared genetic influences, impulsivity, difficulty waiting for rewards, school exclusion and social factors probably all contribute. Mendelian randomisation studies, which use genetic variants as a natural experiment, suggest that genetic liability to ADHD raises the chance of starting smoking and cannabis use, with weaker evidence for alcohol dependence [10, 11]. These studies assume the variants act only through ADHD, which may not hold, and they cannot test the popular "self-medication" idea, for which no good test was found; shared genetics are described on [genes and inheritance](../02-whats-happening-in-my-brain/genes-and-inheritance.md).
- **Short-term and long-term findings differ.** Fewer substance problems during medicated months [7, 8] is a finding about the same period, not evidence that medication protects against addiction later; long-term follow-ups find no effect either way [4, 5].
- **Registry findings are observational.** Within-person comparisons are strong designs, but something else that changes at the same time as starting medication, such as a new job, more support, or a period of stability, could explain part of the difference.
- **People with a current substance use disorder** are often excluded from medication trials, so evidence on treating ADHD in this group is thinner. This is specialist territory.
- **Cannabis** is widely used by adults with ADHD, but a cannabis-specific pooled figure was not found in the sources checked.

## What this means for you

Having ADHD raises the average risk of problems with nicotine, alcohol and other drugs, and that is worth knowing about. The large studies do not support the fear that prescribed ADHD medication starts people down a path to addiction: long-term follow-ups find no effect either way, and fewer substance problems during medicated periods is a shorter-term finding. Decisions about medication, including when someone also has a substance use problem, are for you and a prescriber.

If substance use is putting you at risk right now, places to get help are listed on the [not medical advice](../08-reading-this-well/not-medical-advice.md) page.
