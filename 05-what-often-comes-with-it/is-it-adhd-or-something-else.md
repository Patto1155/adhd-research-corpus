# Is it ADHD, or could it be something else?

> **Short answer:** It could be either, or both: poor concentration, restlessness, forgetfulness and impulsivity are not unique to ADHD. Anxiety, depression, sleep problems, trauma, autism, bipolar disorder, substance use and some medical conditions such as thyroid problems can cause them, and several often occur alongside ADHD. That is why diagnosis rests on a full assessment of your history, not a checklist.

## Why the overlap matters

ADHD is diagnosed from a pattern: symptoms that started in childhood, appear across different settings, cause real problems, and are not better explained by something else (a summary of the criteria, not a quotation) [1]. A clinician's job is to work out which explanation fits, and whether more than one applies.

Two features help most. **Timing** asks whether this has been there since childhood, or started with a particular event or period. **Course** asks whether it is steady, or comes in episodes.

This page is not a guide for ruling ADHD in or out yourself. It describes what clinicians look for and why, and finding one of these conditions does not rule ADHD out.

## What the evidence shows

**Anxiety and depression are the most common overlaps.** Adults with ADHD have about five times the odds of an anxiety disorder and four and a half times the odds of major depression [2]. Worry can scatter concentration, and depression slows thinking and saps motivation, so both can look like inattention. A key question is whether the attention problems were there before the anxiety or low mood began.

Details are on [Anxiety and depression with ADHD: why are they so common?](anxiety-and-depression.md)

**Sleep problems can produce ADHD-like behaviour.** In a randomised trial of 464 children aged 5 to 9 with obstructive sleep apnoea (breathing repeatedly interrupted during sleep), surgery to remove the tonsils and adenoids improved behaviour as rated by caregivers and teachers, and improved quality of life [3]. Scores on formal tests of attention and executive function did not improve [3]. This shows that a sleep disorder can produce ADHD-like behaviour that improves when sleep is treated.

That trial was in children; how often sleep disorders are mistaken for ADHD in adults is less clear. Sleep differences are also part of ADHD itself; see [sleep and the body clock](../03-why-i-think-and-feel-this-way/sleep-and-the-body-clock.md).

**Trauma and PTSD overlap in both directions.** A meta-analysis of 22 studies found post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) was about 2.9 times as common in people with ADHD (a relative risk), and ADHD about 1.7 times as common in people with PTSD [4]. In studies whose comparison group had also been through trauma, the PTSD link was smaller (1.6) [4]. Hypervigilance, poor sleep and difficulty concentrating occur in PTSD and can resemble ADHD; the timing relative to the trauma is an important clue.

**Bipolar disorder is less common but important to distinguish.** A meta-analysis of 71 studies estimated that about 1 in 13 adults with ADHD (7.95%) also has bipolar disorder, and about 1 in 6 adults with bipolar disorder (17%) has ADHD [5]. The distinction matters because the order and type of treatment differ, and the authors stressed awareness of the overlap to avoid misdiagnosis [5].

Bipolar disorder usually comes in distinct episodes of mood change lasting days or weeks, with periods of normal mood in between, while ADHD is more constant. When both are present, ADHD symptoms are still apparent between mood episodes [6]. Borderline personality disorder also shares impulsivity and emotional swings with ADHD [6].

**Autism shares several traits.** About one in five children with ADHD meet criteria for autism, and both can be diagnosed together; see [Autism and ADHD: can you have both?](autism-and-adhd.md)

**Substance use can mimic or mask ADHD.** Heavy use of alcohol, cannabis or stimulants, and withdrawal from them, affect attention and impulsivity. In a long-term study that reassessed apparent late-onset ADHD, most cases were excluded on careful assessment, most often because symptoms occurred only during heavy substance use [7]; see [Addiction and ADHD: is risk higher, and do medicines raise it?](alcohol-drugs-and-addiction.md)

**Medical causes need checking.** Some medical conditions can produce symptoms that resemble ADHD, particularly inattention, and one review recommends a medical evaluation before ADHD is diagnosed [8]. Thyroid problems are a commonly given example: an overactive thyroid can cause restlessness, anxiety and poor concentration, and an underactive one slowed thinking and tiredness. Medicines, and hearing or vision problems, can also affect attention.

That review was selective rather than systematic, and no study of how often medical conditions are mistaken for ADHD was found for this page, so how common this is cannot be stated. Having one of these conditions does not rule ADHD out, and ruling them out does not confirm it. Sorting this out is the job of the assessment, not of a checklist.

## What is uncertain or debated

- **How often each lookalike is misdiagnosed as ADHD,** or ADHD missed because of a lookalike, is not well measured in adults.
- **Adult assessment relies on memory.** Establishing childhood onset decades later depends on recall and old records, which can be patchy. This is part of the adult-onset debate; see [diagnosed as an adult](../01-is-this-real/diagnosed-as-an-adult.md).
- **Overlap or misclassification?** High co-occurrence partly reflects real shared risk and partly symptom lists that overlap. Separating the two is hard.
- **Emotional symptoms** can belong to ADHD, mood disorders or personality, and experts disagree on where the lines fall; see [emotions and rejection](../03-why-i-think-and-feel-this-way/emotions-and-rejection.md).

## What this means for you

If you recognise yourself in ADHD descriptions, that is a reasonable starting point for an assessment, not a conclusion. If you already have a diagnosis of anxiety, depression or another condition, that does not rule ADHD out, and the reverse is also true. The overlap is the reason a full assessment takes time and asks about your whole history.
