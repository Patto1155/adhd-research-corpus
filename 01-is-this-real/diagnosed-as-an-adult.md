# Diagnosed as an adult: how was my ADHD missed, and is it the same?

> **Short answer:** Experts describe adult ADHD as often impairing and under-diagnosed; quiet childhood symptoms are easily missed. Whether all adult cases began in childhood is debated: several population studies found most adults with ADHD had not met criteria as children, but closer checking suggests most began in adolescence or were missed. Either way, adult difficulties are real.

## What the evidence shows

**Adult ADHD is common and often impairing.** A global meta-analysis estimated that about 2.6% of adults have ADHD that began in childhood and persists, and about 6.8% have symptoms at diagnostic level without checking childhood history [1]. A European expert consensus describes adult ADHD as often lifelong, impairing and under-diagnosed in Europe [2].

**Childhood ADHD can be missed for several reasons.** Inattentive symptoms are quieter than hyperactive ones, and structure at home or school can hide difficulties. Some people compensate until demands rise, at university, at work or in parenting.

**Childhood ADHD often continues, but in shifting form.** In a meta-analysis of follow-up studies, about 15% of children with ADHD still met full criteria at age 25, while about 65% did if people with persisting impairing symptoms below the full threshold were counted [3]. In the US Multimodal Treatment Study (MTA) cohort followed to age 25, most participants had a fluctuating course, moving in and out of the diagnostic threshold over the years [4].

**Other cohorts found fluctuation less often.** A study of three other US cohorts, counting a course as fluctuating after two or more switches in and out of diagnosis, found it in a smaller share (17–29%) of young people with ADHD, and never as the largest group [5]. The detail is on [how ADHD changes over life](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/how-adhd-changes-over-life.md).

**Population cohorts raised the "late-onset" question.** In the Dunedin study, which followed 1,037 New Zealanders from birth to age 38, about 6% had ADHD in childhood and about 3% in adulthood, but the two groups barely overlapped: 90% of adults with ADHD had no childhood diagnosis [6]. The adult group also lacked the raised genetic risk and childhood test-score differences seen in the childhood group, leading the authors to ask whether adult ADHD might be a different condition [6].

**Two more cohorts found the same pattern.** In the E-Risk study of 2,040 twins born in England and Wales, 112 of the 166 young people with ADHD at age 18 (67.5%) had not met criteria at any of four childhood assessments [7]. In a Brazilian birth cohort (Pelotas), only 12.6% of young adults with ADHD had had it at age 11, and the authors proposed two separate syndromes [8]. Pelotas measured childhood ADHD with a screening questionnaire rather than a full interview, which may have missed childhood cases [8].

**Closer checking explained away most late cases.** Researchers re-examined 239 children *without* ADHD from the MTA comparison group across eight assessments up to age 25 [9]. About 95% of those who screened positive on symptom checklists in adolescence or adulthood were excluded on closer assessment, most often because symptoms appeared only during heavy substance use, or were better explained by another condition [9]. Remaining late cases mostly began in adolescence, and the study found no adult-onset ADHD independent of a complex psychiatric history [9].

**Re-analysing the same cohorts shrinks the late group too.** When the Pelotas team followed their cohort to age 22 and traced each person's symptoms over time, 78% of adults with ADHD had a trajectory of persistent symptoms from childhood [10]. In a UK cohort, late-onset ADHD rated by parents resembled childhood-onset ADHD in genetic risk scores and test performance, which fits a delayed appearance (perhaps held back by childhood advantages) rather than a different condition; self-rated late-onset ADHD did not [11].

**The leading interpretation is "mostly adolescent onset, or missed", but it is not settled.** A review concluded that a significant share of young adults with ADHD would not have met full criteria as children, but that many had some childhood symptoms or behaviour problems, and most late cases emerged between 12 and 16 [12]. Impairing ADHD can emerge after 12, "although perhaps rarely" with no earlier signs at all [12].

## What is uncertain or debated

**Whether true adult onset exists.** How much "late onset" a study finds depends on its method: cohorts with few childhood assessments, screening questionnaires or adult self-report find large late groups [6], [7], [8], while dense repeated assessment that rules out substance use and other conditions finds very few [9]. The data spanning young adulthood to older age are sparse, and it is unknown whether early-onset and later-onset cases share the same genetic and brain basis [12]. DSM-5-TR still requires several symptoms before age 12.

**How accurate recall is.** Adults reporting their own childhood tend to under-report or reconstruct symptoms, and parents and old school reports are not always available. Changes in who is rating (parent, teacher, self) across studies can manufacture apparent late onset [12].

**How much is masked rather than absent.** High ability, supportive structure or effortful coping may hide symptoms until university, work or parenting raise demands; clinicians call this *compensation*. No study found for this page measures how often compensation delays diagnosis in adults generally. The expert consensus on women and girls lists it as one reason for missed diagnosis, as [Why are girls and women with ADHD so often missed?](women-and-girls.md) explains.

**Treatment response in late-presenting adults** has been studied little [12].

## What this means for you

Being diagnosed in adulthood is common and is not, in itself, a sign of a wrong diagnosis. The late-onset debate is about classification and biology at the group level; it does not decide whether your difficulties are real or whether support helps. A thorough adult assessment considers childhood history *and* other explanations, which is why the process can feel long.
