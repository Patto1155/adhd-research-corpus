# Why are ADHD diagnoses rising, and is it a fad?

> **Short answer:** Studies over three decades found no rise in the share of children meeting ADHD criteria, while diagnoses rose sharply, in England especially since 2020, and recorded rates there are still below expected levels. Some of the rise reflects people once missed. Some is noise: the youngest in a class are diagnosed more often, and milder cases seem to be added.

## What the evidence shows

**True prevalence in children looks stable.** A meta-regression of 135 community studies from around the world over three decades found no increase in the proportion of children who meet ADHD criteria when standardised methods are used [1]. Differences between studies were explained by how they measured ADHD (which criteria, whose reports, whether impairment was required), not by the year or country [1].

**A Swedish twin study saw the same gap between symptoms and diagnoses.** Among 19,271 nine-year-old twins assessed between 2004 and 2014, the share with diagnosis-level ADHD symptoms by parent report did not change significantly, while clinical ADHD diagnoses in national registers rose more than fivefold [2]. Milder, below-threshold symptoms rose slightly [2]. The authors concluded that the rise in diagnoses probably reflects changes in practice, awareness, access or overdiagnosis, rather than more children having ADHD [2].

**Diagnosed ADHD rose in the US too.** In a nationally representative US survey of 186,457 children and teenagers, the share whose parents reported an ADHD diagnosis rose from 6.1% in 1997–98 to 10.2% in 2015–16, in every age, sex, ethnic and income group [3]. These are parent-reported diagnoses, not checked assessments [3].

**Diagnoses in England rose sharply but remain below expected levels.** In GP records of about 3.5 million people in England in mid-2025, 1.19% had a recorded ADHD diagnosis, well below published estimates of how common ADHD is [4]. The shortfall ranged from about 29% in men aged 18 to 24 to about 94% in people aged 65 and over [4]. The authors read this as continued under-recognition, especially in older adults; the figure of 0.05% recorded in over-65s comes from the authors' institutions' press releases, not the abstract [4].

**New diagnoses climbed steeply after 2020.** In records of 42 million patients, yearly rates of new ADHD diagnoses rose markedly from 2000 to 2024, accelerating from 2020 [4]. New diagnoses were highest in boys and young men under 18, and stayed low and stable in people aged 65 and over [4]. The abstract does not give yearly rates, so none are quoted here.

**Relative age shows the process is noisy.** In US states where children start school if they turn five by 1 September, children born in August (the youngest in the class) were diagnosed at 85.1 per 10,000, against 63.6 per 10,000 for September-born children (the oldest) [5]. That is an extra 21.5 diagnoses per 10,000 children in absolute terms (about 0.2 percentage points), or, by this page's calculation, about 34% higher in relative terms [5]. Treatment rates showed a similar gap (52.9 against 40.4 per 10,000) [5].

**The age gap points to immaturity, not to ADHD being unreal.** No such gap appeared in states with other cut-off dates, or for asthma, diabetes or obesity [5]. This suggests ordinary immaturity is sometimes read as ADHD in younger children. It does not show that ADHD is unreal.

**The relative-age effect is found worldwide.** A meta-analysis of 25 studies (about 8 million children) found that children in the youngest third of their school year were about 1.34 times as likely to be diagnosed as their classmates [6]. The meta-analysis did not report an absolute difference; in the US study above it was about 0.2 percentage points [5, 6]. A systematic review of 19 studies in 13 countries found the effect in 17, including countries that prescribe ADHD medicine relatively rarely; two Danish studies found a weak or no effect [7].

**There is evidence of expansion into milder cases in children.** A scoping review of 334 studies found evidence consistent with overdiagnosis in children and adolescents: rising diagnosis and prescribing, and 25 studies suggesting the extra cases tend to be milder [8]. Only five studies looked at whether diagnosing those milder cases does more good than harm [8].

**The review's authors judged that milder cases may be overdiagnosed.** They concluded that they had found evidence of overdiagnosis and overtreatment in children and adolescents, and that those five studies supported "diminishing returns", where harms may outweigh benefits for young people with milder symptoms [8]. They also stressed how few studies have measured those benefits and harms directly [8].

**Under-diagnosis and over-diagnosis happen in different groups.** The same system can over-diagnose some children (young-for-year, borderline cases) and under-diagnose others: older adults [4], in the US Asian, Black and Hispanic children, and girls and women, as [Why are girls and women with ADHD so often missed?](women-and-girls.md) explains.

The US figures on ethnicity are on [How is ADHD diagnosed, and why is there no test?](how-diagnosis-works.md)

## What is uncertain or debated

**Why diagnoses jumped after 2020.** Likely contributors include greater public awareness, social media, changes in demand and services during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, and adults seeking assessment after a child's diagnosis. The England study describes the trend but cannot separate these causes [4]. Social media content about ADHD is often inaccurate, which may push both appropriate and inappropriate referrals.

**Whether "overdiagnosis" is the right word.** Some researchers argue that the threshold for a dimensional condition is a judgement, so "milder cases" may be legitimate diagnoses that bring benefit. Others argue that labelling and medicating milder cases has unproven benefit and real costs. The Kazda review leans towards the second view for milder cases, but the direct evidence on benefits and harms that would settle it comes from only a handful of studies [8].

**Adult diagnoses have been studied less.** Most overdiagnosis research is in children. Evidence on whether adult diagnoses are accurate, and on private or online assessment services, is limited.

**Benchmarks are estimates.** The England comparison uses prevalence figures from other studies, not a measure of how many people in England have ADHD, so the size of the gap is approximate [4].

## What this means for you

If you were diagnosed recently, the rise in diagnoses is not evidence that yours is wrong; much of it reflects people who would have been missed in earlier decades. The relative-age and mild-case evidence is a reason for diagnosis to be careful and thorough, which protects people who do have ADHD as much as those who don't.

If you are sceptical of the trend, the stable prevalence data are the best answer to "it's a fad": the condition did not become more common, recognition did. Both things are true at once: some groups are still missed while some milder cases are added.
