# Why are ADHD diagnoses rising, and is it a fad?

> **Short answer:** Studies over three decades found no rise in the share of children meeting ADHD criteria; diagnoses have risen, and press releases on an England study say especially since 2020, with recorded rates still below expected levels. Some reflects people once missed. Some is noise: the youngest in a class are diagnosed more often, and milder cases seem to be added.

## What the evidence shows

**True prevalence in children looks stable.** A meta-regression of community studies from around the world over three decades found no increase in the proportion of children who meet ADHD criteria when standardised methods are used [1]. Differences between studies were explained by how they measured ADHD (which criteria, whose reports, whether impairment was required), not by the year or country [1].

**Diagnoses in England rose sharply but remain below expected levels.** In GP records of more than 3.5 million people in England in 2025, 1.19% had an ADHD diagnosis, against benchmarks of 3–5%; among people over 65, only 0.05% had a recorded diagnosis [2]. These two figures come from press releases by the authors' institutions and matching press coverage, because the paper's own abstract could not be checked. The authors read them as continued under-recognition, especially in older adults.

**New diagnoses climbed steeply after 2020.** The England study shows the rise [2], but its yearly rates circulate only in press coverage, so they are not quoted here. The rise has been most marked in adults and women.

**Relative age shows the process is noisy.** In US states where children start school if they turn five by 1 September, children born in August (the youngest in the class) were diagnosed at 85.1 per 10,000, against 63.6 per 10,000 for September-born children (the oldest) [3]. By our calculation from those published rates, that is about 34% higher in relative terms, or an extra 21 to 22 diagnoses per 10,000 children (about 0.2 percentage points) in absolute terms. The two rates were seen only in a secondary summary of the paper; the treatment figures in the paper's abstract are consistent with them.

**The age gap points to immaturity, not to ADHD being unreal.** No such gap appeared in states with other cut-off dates, or for asthma or diabetes [3]. This suggests ordinary immaturity is sometimes read as ADHD in younger children. It does not show that ADHD is unreal.

**There is evidence of expansion into milder cases in children.** A scoping review of 334 studies found evidence consistent with overdiagnosis in children and adolescents: rising diagnosis and prescribing, and 25 studies suggesting the extra cases tend to be milder [4]. Only five studies looked at whether diagnosing those milder cases does more good than harm [4].

**The review's authors judged that milder cases may be overdiagnosed.** It concluded that there is "convincing evidence" that ADHD is overdiagnosed and overtreated in children and adolescents, and that for people with milder symptoms the harms of a diagnosis may often outweigh the benefits [4]. It also stressed how few studies have measured those benefits and harms directly [4].

**Under-diagnosis and over-diagnosis happen in different groups.** The same system can over-diagnose some children (young-for-year, borderline cases) and under-diagnose others: older adults [2], in the US Asian, Black and Hispanic children, and girls and women, as [Why are girls and women with ADHD so often missed?](women-and-girls.md) explains.

The US figures on ethnicity are on [How is ADHD diagnosed, and why is there no test?](how-diagnosis-works.md)

## What is uncertain or debated

**Why diagnoses jumped after 2020.** Likely contributors include greater public awareness, social media, changes in demand and services during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, and adults seeking assessment after a child's diagnosis. The England study describes the trend but cannot separate these causes [2]. Social media content about ADHD is often inaccurate, which may push both appropriate and inappropriate referrals.

**Whether "overdiagnosis" is the right word.** Some researchers argue that the threshold for a dimensional condition is a judgement, so "milder cases" may be legitimate diagnoses that bring benefit. Others argue that labelling and medicating milder cases has unproven benefit and real costs. The Kazda review leans towards the second view for milder cases, but the direct evidence on benefits and harms that would settle it comes from only a handful of studies [4].

**Adult diagnoses have been studied less.** Most overdiagnosis research is in children. Evidence on whether adult diagnoses are accurate, and on private or online assessment services, is limited.

**Benchmarks are estimates.** The 3–5% comparison for England is a range from other studies, not a precise measure of how many people in England have ADHD [2].

## What this means for you

If you were diagnosed recently, the rise in diagnoses is not evidence that yours is wrong; much of it reflects people who would have been missed in earlier decades. The relative-age and mild-case evidence is a reason for diagnosis to be careful and thorough, which protects people who do have ADHD as much as those who don't.

If you are sceptical of the trend, the stable prevalence data are the best answer to "it's a fad": the condition did not become more common, recognition did. Both things are true at once: some groups are still missed while some milder cases are added.
