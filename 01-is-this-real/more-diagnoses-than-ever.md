# Why are so many more people being diagnosed? Is ADHD a fad?

> **Short answer:** The share of children who actually meet ADHD criteria, measured the same way, has not risen over three decades. What has risen is how many people are *diagnosed*, especially adults and women since about 2020. Some of that rise is catching up on people who were missed, and in England recorded diagnoses are still well below expected rates. Some of it is noise in the diagnostic process: there is good evidence that the youngest children in a school year are diagnosed more often, and that milder cases are being added. Both things are true at once.

## What the evidence shows

**True prevalence in children looks stable.** A meta-regression of community studies from around the world over three decades found no increase in the proportion of children who meet ADHD criteria when standardised methods are used [1]. Differences between studies were explained by how they measured ADHD (which criteria, whose reports, whether impairment was required), not by the year or country [1].

**Diagnoses in England rose sharply but remain below expected levels.** In GP records of more than 3.5 million people in England in 2025, 1.19% had an ADHD diagnosis, against benchmarks of 3–5% [2]. New diagnoses rose steeply after 2020 [2]; the yearly rates circulate only in press coverage and are not quoted here. Among people over 65, only 0.05% had a recorded diagnosis [2]. The authors read this as continued under-recognition, especially in older adults.

**Relative age shows the process is noisy.** In US states where children start school if they turn five by 1 September, children born in August (the youngest in the class) were diagnosed at 85.1 per 10,000, against 63.6 per 10,000 for September-born children (the oldest) [3]. By our calculation from those published rates, that is about 34% higher in relative terms, or an extra 21 to 22 diagnoses per 10,000 children (about 0.2 percentage points) in absolute terms. No such gap appeared in states with other cut-off dates, or for asthma or diabetes [3]. This suggests ordinary immaturity is sometimes read as ADHD in younger children. It does not show that ADHD is unreal.

**There is evidence of expansion into milder cases in children.** A scoping review of 334 studies found evidence consistent with overdiagnosis in children and adolescents: rising diagnosis and prescribing, and 25 studies suggesting the extra cases tend to be milder [4]. Only five studies looked at whether diagnosing those milder cases does more good than harm [4]. The review concluded that there is "convincing evidence" that ADHD is overdiagnosed and overtreated in children and adolescents, and that for people with milder symptoms the harms of a diagnosis may often outweigh the benefits; it also stressed how few studies have measured those benefits and harms directly [4].

**Under-diagnosis and over-diagnosis happen in different groups.** The same system can over-diagnose some children (young-for-year, borderline cases) and under-diagnose others: girls and women ([women and girls](women-and-girls.md)), older adults [2], and in the US, Asian, Black and Hispanic children ([how diagnosis works](how-diagnosis-works.md)).

## What is uncertain or debated

**Why diagnoses jumped after 2020.** Likely contributors include greater public awareness, social media, changes in demand and services during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, and adults seeking assessment after a child's diagnosis. The England study describes the trend but cannot separate these causes [2]. Social media content about ADHD is often inaccurate, which may push both appropriate and inappropriate referrals.

**Whether "overdiagnosis" is the right word.** Some researchers argue that the threshold for a dimensional condition is a judgement, so "milder cases" may be legitimate diagnoses that bring benefit. Others argue that labelling and medicating milder cases has unproven benefit and real costs. The Kazda review leans towards the second view for milder cases, but the direct evidence on benefits and harms that would settle it comes from only a handful of studies [4].

**Adult diagnoses have been studied less.** Most overdiagnosis research is in children. Evidence on whether adult diagnoses are accurate, and on private or online assessment services, is limited.

**Benchmarks are estimates.** The 3–5% comparison for England is a range from other studies, not a precise measure of how many people in England have ADHD [2].

## What this means for you

If you were diagnosed recently, the rise in diagnoses is not evidence that yours is wrong; much of it reflects people who would have been missed in earlier decades. The relative-age and mild-case evidence is a reason for diagnosis to be careful and thorough, which protects people who do have ADHD as much as those who don't. If you are sceptical of the trend, the stable prevalence data are the best answer to "it's a fad": the condition did not become more common, recognition did.
