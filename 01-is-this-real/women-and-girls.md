# Why are girls and women with ADHD so often missed?

> **Short answer:** ADHD is diagnosed more often in boys than girls, but the gap narrows in adulthood, and registry studies show that girls and women are diagnosed years later on average. Expert consensus attributes this to differences in how ADHD tends to show up, to clinicians' and teachers' expectations, to co-occurring anxiety or depression drawing attention first, and to effortful compensation. When girls and boys are assessed with full clinical interviews, symptom severity looks similar. Evidence on hormones is suggestive but thin.

## What the evidence shows

**Boys are diagnosed more often in childhood.** In a meta-analysis of 86 child and adolescent studies, ADHD was more common in boys, with a male-to-female ratio of roughly 3 to 1 in the best-estimate studies [1]. <!-- CHECK: the 3.2:1 ratio was read from a garbled table excerpt in search results; confirm in Willcutt 2012 Table 1. The narrowing to about 1.5–2:1 in adults (scoping item X2) has no checked primary source and is not quoted here --> In adulthood the difference between sexes is smaller; the Dunedin birth cohort, for example, found adult ADHD roughly balanced between men and women [2].

**Girls and women are diagnosed later.** In Stockholm County, among 85,330 people with ADHD, the median age at diagnosis was 19 for females and 14 for males, a gap of about four to five years [3]. Females also had more psychiatric diagnoses and more contact with health services *before* their ADHD diagnosis, which suggests they were seeking help for something but the ADHD was not recognised [3]. In a nationwide Danish cohort, new ADHD diagnoses peaked at age 8 in boys and at age 17 in girls [4].

**Symptom severity looks similar when assessment is thorough.** A meta-analysis of 52 studies (8,423 females and 9,985 males with ADHD) found no significant sex difference in core symptom severity on clinical interviews, in children or adults [5]. Differences appeared on rating scales, which may reflect who fills them in and what they expect to see [5]. <!-- CHECK: the direction and size of the rating-scale differences were not captured; confirm in Young 2024 full text -->

**Experts point to several reasons for under-recognition.** An international expert consensus listed: presentations with more inattention and less disruptive behaviour, gender stereotypes held by teachers, parents and clinicians, co-occurring anxiety, depression or eating problems being diagnosed instead, and girls' efforts to compensate or mask (hide difficulties to fit in) [6]. The consensus recommends a lifespan approach that considers female-specific issues [6]. These are expert conclusions, not measured effect sizes.

**Excess risks are not smaller in women.** Some large registry studies find that the gap in health outcomes between people with and without ADHD is as large or larger for women. <!-- CHECK: basis is Dalsgaard 2015 (Danish mortality, MRR 2.85 women vs 1.27 men, comorbidity-excluded), per scoping item X6; confirm the home page reports it --> The numbers, with absolute risks, are on [the hard statistics in context](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/the-hard-statistics-in-context.md).

## What is uncertain or debated

**How much of the sex ratio is real.** Some of the childhood gap probably reflects a real difference in how often hyperactivity occurs, and some reflects referral bias. Studies cannot cleanly separate the two.

**What "masking" is and how to measure it.** The term is widely used and fits many women's accounts, but there is no validated measure of ADHD masking, and its size has not been quantified.

**Hormones.** A 2025 systematic review found only 11 studies on sex hormones and ADHD symptoms in females, and their findings were "largely suggestive" of links during puberty and the menstrual cycle [7]. Evidence on perimenopause and menopause is almost absent. The detail is on [hormones, cycles and menopause](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/hormones-cycles-and-menopause.md).

**Gender-diverse people** are largely absent from this research. One large study found higher self-reported rates of several diagnoses, including ADHD, in transgender and gender-diverse people, but little is known about how ADHD presents or is assessed in these groups ([how diagnosis works](how-diagnosis-works.md)).

## What this means for you

If you are a woman diagnosed in adulthood after years of being treated for anxiety or depression, the Swedish data describe a common path, not a personal failure to notice. Late diagnosis does not mean milder ADHD; on thorough assessment, severity looks similar across sexes. What the research cannot yet tell you is how your hormones will interact with your symptoms, because that has barely been studied.
