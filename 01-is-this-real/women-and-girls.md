# Why are girls and women with ADHD so often missed?

> **Short answer:** Registry studies show girls and women with ADHD are diagnosed years later than males on average. Expert consensus links this to how ADHD tends to show up, to others' expectations, to anxiety or depression being noticed first, and to compensation. On full clinical interviews, symptom severity looks similar across sexes; evidence on hormones is suggestive but thin.

One section below gives death statistics.

## What the evidence shows

**Boys are diagnosed more often in childhood.** In a meta-analysis of 86 child and adolescent studies, ADHD was more common in boys than girls [1]. A checked figure for the size of the ratio was not found, so none is quoted here. In adulthood the difference between sexes is smaller; the Dunedin birth cohort, for example, found adult ADHD roughly balanced between men and women [2].

**Girls and women are diagnosed later.** In Stockholm County, among 85,330 people with ADHD, females were diagnosed about four years later than males on average [3]. Females also had more psychiatric diagnoses and more contact with health services *before* their ADHD diagnosis, which suggests they were seeking help for something but the ADHD was not recognised [3]. In a nationwide Danish cohort, new ADHD diagnoses peaked at age 8 in boys and at age 17 in girls [4].

**Symptom severity looks similar when assessment is thorough.** A meta-analysis of 52 studies (8,423 females and 9,985 males with ADHD) found no significant sex difference in core symptom severity on clinical interviews, in children or adults [5]. Some small differences appeared on other measures (for example, adult men were rated as more inattentive than adult women), which may reflect who does the rating and what they expect to see [5].

**Experts point to several reasons for under-recognition.** An international expert consensus listed presentations with more inattention and less disruptive behaviour; gender stereotypes held by teachers, parents and clinicians; co-occurring anxiety, depression or eating problems being diagnosed instead; and girls' efforts to compensate or mask (hide difficulties to fit in) [6]. The consensus recommends a lifespan approach that considers female-specific issues [6]. These are expert conclusions, not measured effect sizes.

**The raised death rate may be larger in women, though the estimate rests on few deaths.** In a Danish nationwide cohort, after people with oppositional, conduct or substance use disorders were excluded, ADHD was linked with a higher death rate in girls and women than in boys and men [7]. The mortality rate ratio (how many times higher the death rate was than in people without ADHD) was 2.85 for girls and women, with a 95% CI (the range the true figure probably lies in) of 1.56 to 4.71; for boys and men it was 1.27 (0.89 to 1.76, a range that includes no difference) [7].

In absolute terms, across everyone in the study, people with ADHD died at a rate of 5.85 per 10,000 person-years (one person followed for one year), against 2.21 without ADHD [7]. Separate absolute rates for each sex were not found for this page. Deaths in this young cohort were mostly from accidents, and more is in [the hard statistics in context](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/the-hard-statistics-in-context.md).

## What is uncertain or debated

**How much of the sex ratio is real.** Some of the childhood gap probably reflects a real difference in how often hyperactivity occurs, and some reflects referral bias. Studies cannot cleanly separate the two.

**What "masking" is and how to measure it.** The term is widely used and fits many women's accounts, but there is no validated measure of ADHD masking, and its size has not been quantified.

**Hormones.** A 2025 systematic review found only 11 studies on sex hormones and ADHD symptoms in females, and their findings were "largely suggestive" of links during puberty and the menstrual cycle [8]. For perimenopause and menopause, two 2025 comparison studies exist and reached different answers, and neither followed the same women through the transition. The detail is in [hormones, cycles and menopause](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/hormones-cycles-and-menopause.md).

**Gender-diverse people** are largely absent from this research. One large study found higher self-reported rates of several diagnoses, including ADHD, in transgender and gender-diverse people, but little is known about how ADHD presents or is assessed in these groups. The study is described in [How is ADHD diagnosed, and why is there no test?](how-diagnosis-works.md)

## What this means for you

If you are a woman diagnosed in adulthood after years of being treated for anxiety or depression, the Swedish data describe a common path, not a personal failure to notice. Late diagnosis does not mean milder ADHD; on thorough assessment, severity looks similar across sexes. What the research cannot yet tell you is how your hormones will interact with your symptoms, because the studies so far are few and small.
