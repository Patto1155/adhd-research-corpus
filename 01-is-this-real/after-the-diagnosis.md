# I've just been diagnosed as an adult. Why do I feel relieved, sad and doubtful all at once?

> **Short answer:** Interview studies of adults diagnosed later in life find that most people describe a mix: relief that there is an explanation, regret about years of struggle that might have been avoided, and some doubt about whether the label is right. Many then rework how they understand their past and themselves. This research is small and qualitative, so it shows what these experiences are like and how common the themes are across studies, not how many people feel each one. Some people come to see ADHD mainly as a medical condition, others mainly as a neurological difference, and many use both; research does not settle which is right.

## What the evidence shows

**Relief and burden often arrive together.** A 2022 synthesis of 7 qualitative studies of adults diagnosed in adulthood found two main themes: ADHD as part of identity, and ADHD as a diagnosis [1]. Its subthemes included identity before and after diagnosis, an "unnecessary struggle" in the years before, and the diagnosis as both a revelation and a burden [1]. The authors concluded that delayed diagnosis caused avoidable suffering [1].

**A larger review exists, but its findings could not be checked.** A 2026 systematic review synthesised qualitative studies of adults who received an ADHD diagnosis in adulthood [2]. Its abstract could not be reached for this page, and the themes reported for it so far come only from magazine summaries, so they are not given here as its findings.

**Most people in one interview study valued the diagnosis, but not all.** A Swedish study interviewed 21 adults diagnosed with ADHD [3]. Positive experiences dominated: the diagnosis explained a previously puzzling life history and gave self-knowledge [3]. Some participants also felt it could restrict their options, felt let down by a lack of professional help, or questioned whether the diagnosis was right [3].

**Doubt is reported, but has barely been studied directly.** The "am I making this up?" feeling appears in the Swedish interviews [3] and in broader reviews of adults' experience [4]. No study found for this page focused on self-doubt after diagnosis. One likely source is outside the person: in a German study, the stereotype adults with ADHD most often perceived in the public was doubt that ADHD is a genuine disorder (see [stigma and telling people](../07-family-work-and-other-people/stigma-and-telling-people.md)).

**Identifying strongly with ADHD is not, on average, linked to better or worse mental health.** In a UK survey of 319 adults with ADHD, 77% preferred person-first wording ("person with ADHD") [5]. People who identified more strongly with ADHD were more likely to prefer identity-first wording ("ADHDer") and to take medication [5]. Contrary to the authors' prediction, strength of identification was not linked to self-esteem, anxiety or depression [5]. The survey was a single snapshot, so it cannot show cause and effect.

**Some adults describe strengths, in small studies.** Interviews with 6 successful men with ADHD in London produced themes of "cognitive dynamism" (constant mental activity, curiosity, creativity), courage, energy, humanity, resilience and transcendence [6]. Six men chosen for success cannot show how common these traits are among adults with ADHD generally.

### Two ways of making sense of ADHD

**The medical framing** treats ADHD as a condition: symptoms causing impairment, which can be diagnosed and treated. It brings access to assessment, treatment and legal protection, and a large evidence base ([what ADHD is](what-adhd-is.md)).

**The neurodiversity framing** treats ADHD as natural variation in how brains work, and places much of the difficulty in environments built for other minds. It brings self-respect, community, and attention to changing surroundings, not only the person.

**They can be held together.** Sonuga-Barke and Thapar argue that clinicians and researchers can take on neurodiversity ideas while recognising that diagnosis and treatment help many people [7]. An interview study of people with ADHD, published in a Canadian disability-studies journal, found a range of views: participants who felt connected with neurodiversity described it as an empowering way to organise their lives, while others related to it less [8].

### Religious and spiritual life

**There is very little research, and none on meaning or purpose after diagnosis.** In a US cohort of more than 8,000 people followed from age 12 to 34, more ADHD symptoms went with lower religious involvement at nearly every stage [9]. In an Israeli survey of 806 Jewish adults, religious observance went with fewer ADHD-related problems and less distress [10]. Both are observational, and neither tells you whether faith helps or ADHD makes religious practice harder. No study was found of how adults with ADHD find meaning, purpose or spiritual wellbeing after diagnosis.

## What is uncertain or debated

**What qualitative research can and cannot tell you.** Qualitative studies collect people's own accounts, usually through interviews, and look for shared themes. They show what an experience is like and what matters to people. They cannot say how many people feel relief or grief, whether one reaction leads to better outcomes, or whether findings hold for people unlike those interviewed. Samples are small (6 to 21 people in the single studies here that report a size) and made up of volunteers willing to talk, mostly in Europe or North America.

**How long these reactions last.** The studies are snapshots or single interviews. Nothing found here followed newly diagnosed adults over years to see whether grief fades, whether identity settles, or what predicts either.

**Which framing is better for people.** The one survey found here did not find that identifying with ADHD protects mental health [5], but it was not designed to test the neurodiversity framing as such. This is partly a question of values, which data alone cannot answer.

## What this means for you

Feeling relieved, regretting lost years and doubting all in the same week is what adults describe in these studies, not a sign that something is wrong with the diagnosis or with you. Doubt about whether ADHD is "real" is also something the wider public expresses, and some of what feels like inner doubt may be absorbed from it. How you come to describe ADHD to yourself, as a condition, a difference or both, is your own decision; the research describes the options without ranking them. If the grief becomes heavy or does not lift, [this page](../08-reading-this-well/not-medical-advice.md) lists where to get support.
