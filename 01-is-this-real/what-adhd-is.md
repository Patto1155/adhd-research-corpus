# What is ADHD, actually?

> **Short answer:** ADHD (attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder) is a neurodevelopmental condition: a pattern of inattention, hyperactivity or impulsivity that starts in childhood, shows up across different parts of life, and causes real difficulty. The traits it describes exist in everyone to some degree, like height or blood pressure, and a diagnosis marks the point where they cause lasting problems. It is one of the most studied conditions in psychiatry, and the evidence that it is real, common and often impairing is strong. What it is *not* is a single brain defect, a single gene, or a lab-test result.

## What the evidence shows

**It is common and found worldwide.** Pooled community surveys put ADHD in roughly 5–7% of children and adolescents, using standard diagnostic criteria; the World Federation of ADHD consensus gives 5.9% of young people [1][2]. Among adults, about 2.6% have ADHD that started in childhood and persists, and about 6.8% have current symptoms at a level that meets criteria, though the second figure includes people whose childhood history was not checked [3]. Rates are similar across regions once you account for how each study measured ADHD [2].

**It is dimensional, not a separate category of person.** ADHD traits are spread continuously across the population. The term for this is *dimensional*. There is no natural gap between "has it" and "doesn't". The diagnosis is a threshold set on symptoms *plus* impairment (meaning the symptoms cause problems at school, work, home or in relationships) [1]. A threshold on a continuum does not make the condition unreal: high blood pressure is diagnosed the same way.

**It runs strongly in families, mostly through genes.** Twin studies estimate that roughly three-quarters of the *variation* in ADHD between people is explained by genetic differences. ADHD is "highly heritable and multifactorial" [4]. Hundreds or thousands of common genetic variants each add a tiny amount of risk; there is no "ADHD gene". The full numbers and what heritability does and does not mean are on [genes and inheritance](../02-whats-happening-in-my-brain/genes-and-inheritance.md).

**It is linked to measurable outcomes.** The World Federation of ADHD consensus, which drew only on meta-analyses or studies of more than 2,000 people, concluded that ADHD is associated on average with worse outcomes in education, work, injury, and physical and mental health, and that these associations are found in many countries [1]. These are group averages. Most people with ADHD do not experience most of these outcomes, and some are linked to things that can change, such as untreated symptoms or co-occurring conditions. [The hard statistics in context](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/the-hard-statistics-in-context.md) gives the absolute numbers.

**It has three presentations.** The diagnostic manuals describe a mainly inattentive presentation, a mainly hyperactive-impulsive presentation, and a combined one [5]. Since DSM-5 (2013) these have been called "presentations" rather than "types", because a person's current symptom profile can change over time [5].

## What is uncertain or debated

**Where exactly the threshold should sit.** Because ADHD is dimensional, any cut-off is partly a judgement. Researchers argue about whether milder cases near the threshold benefit from diagnosis; see [more diagnoses than ever](more-diagnoses-than-ever.md).

**What it is at the level of biology.** Brain imaging and genetics show small average differences between groups, but no single mechanism explains ADHD, and no biological test can diagnose it [4]. People who share the diagnosis can differ a lot in their underlying profile; see [why no two ADHD brains are alike](../02-whats-happening-in-my-brain/why-no-two-adhd-brains-are-alike.md).

**Disorder or difference.** Some people prefer to describe ADHD as a form of neurodiversity (natural variation in how brains work) rather than a disorder. Sonuga-Barke and Thapar argue the two framings are compatible: one can respect neurodivergent identity while recognising that diagnosis and treatment help many people [6]. The evidence does not settle this, because it is partly a question of values.

**Whether adult-onset ADHD is the same condition.** Some adults meet criteria without a clear childhood history. Whether these are missed childhood cases or something different is unresolved; see [diagnosed as an adult](diagnosed-as-an-adult.md).

## What this means for you

If you or someone close to you has ADHD, the research supports treating it as a real condition with real effects, not as laziness or a character flaw. It also supports seeing it as a difference in degree rather than a different kind of person. The group-level findings on this page cannot tell you how ADHD will play out in any one life.
