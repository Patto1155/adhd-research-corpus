# How is ADHD diagnosed, and why is there no test?

> **Short answer:** ADHD is diagnosed by a trained clinician through a detailed assessment, not by a blood test, brain scan or app. The assessment covers symptoms across childhood and adulthood, problems in more than one part of life, and whether something else explains them better. Online quizzes can suggest an assessment is worth having, but cannot make or rule out a diagnosis.

## What the evidence shows

**The criteria are about pattern, onset and impact.** Under DSM-5-TR, the American diagnostic manual, a person needs a set number of symptoms from two lists of nine (inattention, and hyperactivity-impulsivity): six or more for children, five or more from age 17 [1]. Symptoms must have lasted at least six months, several must have been present before age 12, they must show up in two or more settings (such as home and work), and they must clearly interfere with functioning [1]. The international manual, ICD-11, uses a broadly similar description, including onset in childhood and the same main presentations, though its symptom lists differ somewhat from DSM-5-TR's [2].

**Rating scales help, but do not decide.** The English NICE guideline says diagnosis should not rest on rating scales or observation alone, though rating scales are helpful added tools and observation (for example at school) is useful when there is doubt about symptoms [3]. Short screeners, such as the six-question Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS), are built to flag people for assessment. They produce false positives because anxiety, depression, poor sleep and stress all raise scores.

**No scan or biomarker can diagnose ADHD.** Large brain-imaging studies find very small differences between groups: in the largest mega-analysis of subcortical volumes, the biggest difference was a Cohen's d of 0.19 [4]. By our calculation from that published figure, the ADHD and comparison groups overlap by more than 90%, which is far too much to sort one person into a category.

**Guidelines rely on clinical assessment instead.** The biological differences found in research are small averages across groups, not markers that work for one person. Guidelines such as NICE's base diagnosis on a clinical and psychosocial assessment, not on scans or blood tests [3]. The numbers are on [Does ADHD mean my brain is damaged or "three years behind"?](../02-whats-happening-in-my-brain/brain-structure-and-development.md)

**Social media is a poor guide.** Of the 100 most popular ADHD videos on TikTok in one 2022 analysis, about half (52) were rated misleading by clinicians [5]. Many described common human experiences as ADHD symptoms without the impairment and pervasiveness the diagnosis requires.

**Who gets diagnosed differs by ethnicity, at least in the US.** In a US insurance cohort of 238,011 children, Asian, Black and Hispanic children were less likely than White children to be diagnosed with ADHD by age 12, after adjusting for sex, region and income: hazard ratios of 0.48, 0.83 and 0.77 respectively [6]. A hazard ratio of 0.83 means a diagnosis rate about 17% lower. Overall, the estimated share of children diagnosed by age 12 was about 13% (a cumulative incidence; 4.8% of the cohort had a diagnosis during the study's follow-up), but the study's abstract does not give absolute rates for each group [6].

**The gaps look partly like missed diagnoses.** A US survey that followed 4,297 children from age 10 to 16 found persistent gaps in ADHD medication use [7]. It concluded they more likely reflect under-diagnosis and under-treatment of Black and Latino children than over-diagnosis of White children [7]. The evidence suggests the ethnic differences in diagnosis are partly about access and recognition.

**The picture is not simply "minority children are missed".** A meta-analysis of 21 US studies estimated ADHD at about 14.5% in Black children and adults (13.9% in children alone), higher than the general US estimate, with wide variation between studies [8]. Many included samples were from disadvantaged communities, which may raise the estimate. Together these studies suggest both under-recognition in clinics and real uncertainty about how symptoms are rated and measured.

## What is uncertain or debated

**Bias and access are hard to separate.** The US studies cannot fully tell whether lower diagnosis rates come from less access to care, family reluctance shaped by fear of stigma or discrimination, clinicians interpreting the same behaviour differently, or real differences in rates. The Cénat review's high prevalence estimate and the Shi cohort's lower diagnosis rates also measure different things: symptoms in surveys versus diagnoses in insurance claims [6, 8].

**Evidence outside the US is thin.** No comparable large studies of ethnic-minority diagnosis rates in the UK, Australia or Europe were found for this page. Treat the US findings as a reason to ask questions, not as figures that transfer.

**Gender-diverse people.** A study pooling five datasets (641,860 people, 3,777 gender-diverse) found that transgender and gender-diverse people reported higher rates of several diagnoses, including ADHD, than cisgender people [9]. Diagnoses were self-reported, the strongest associations were with autism and depression, and the study cannot say why. Research on how ADHD assessment works for gender-diverse people is very limited.

**Adults recalling childhood.** Adult diagnosis relies on memory of symptoms before 12, often supported by school reports or a parent's account. Recall is imperfect, which feeds the late-onset debate covered in [Diagnosed as an adult: how was my ADHD missed, and is it the same?](diagnosed-as-an-adult.md)

## What this means for you

A careful assessment is a structured clinical judgement, not a test score. That can feel less solid than a blood result, but it is how many well-established conditions are diagnosed. If a questionnaire or video has made you wonder, the useful next step is a proper assessment, which also looks for other explanations.

Information here is not a diagnosis, as [not medical advice](../08-reading-this-well/not-medical-advice.md) explains.
