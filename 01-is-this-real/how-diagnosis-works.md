# How is ADHD diagnosed, and why is there no test?

> **Short answer:** ADHD is diagnosed by a trained clinician through a detailed assessment: a structured history of symptoms across childhood and adulthood, evidence that they cause problems in more than one part of life, and a check that something else does not explain them better. There is no blood test, brain scan or app that can diagnose it, because the biological differences found in research are small averages across groups, not markers that work for one person. Online quizzes and questionnaires can suggest that an assessment is worth having, but they cannot make or rule out a diagnosis. Who gets diagnosed also varies by ethnicity, which the evidence suggests is partly about access and recognition.

## What the evidence shows

**The criteria are about pattern, onset and impact.** Under DSM-5-TR, the American diagnostic manual, a person needs a set number of symptoms from two lists of nine (inattention, and hyperactivity-impulsivity): six or more for children, five or more from age 17 [1]. Symptoms must have lasted at least six months, several must have been present before age 12, they must show up in two or more settings (such as home and work), and they must clearly interfere with functioning [1]. The international manual, ICD-11, uses a broadly similar description, including onset in childhood and the same main presentations, though its symptom lists differ somewhat from DSM-5-TR's [9].

**Rating scales help, but do not decide.** The English NICE guideline says diagnosis should not rest on rating scales or observation alone, though rating scales are helpful added tools and observation (for example at school) is useful when there is doubt about symptoms [2]. Short screeners, such as the six-question Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS), are built to flag people for assessment. They produce false positives because anxiety, depression, poor sleep and stress all raise scores.

**No scan or biomarker can diagnose ADHD.** Large brain-imaging studies find differences between groups that are very small: in the largest mega-analysis of subcortical volumes, the biggest difference was a Cohen's d of 0.19 [3]. By our calculation from that published figure, the ADHD and comparison groups overlap by more than 90%. A difference that small cannot sort one person into a category. Guidelines such as NICE's base diagnosis on a clinical and psychosocial assessment, not on scans or blood tests [2]. The numbers are on [brain structure and development](../02-whats-happening-in-my-brain/brain-structure-and-development.md).

**Social media is a poor guide.** Of the 100 most popular ADHD videos on TikTok in one 2022 analysis, about half (52) were rated misleading by clinicians [4]. Many described common human experiences as ADHD symptoms without the impairment and pervasiveness the diagnosis requires.

**Who gets diagnosed differs by ethnicity, at least in the US.** In a US insurance cohort of 238,011 children, Asian, Black and Hispanic children were less likely than White children to be diagnosed with ADHD by age 12, after adjusting for sex, region and income: hazard ratios of 0.48, 0.83 and 0.77 respectively [5]. A hazard ratio of 0.83 means a diagnosis rate about 17% lower. Overall, the estimated share of children diagnosed by age 12 was about 13% (a cumulative incidence; 4.8% of the cohort had a diagnosis during the study's follow-up), but the study's abstract does not give absolute rates for each group. A US survey that followed 4,297 children from age 10 to 16 found persistent gaps in ADHD medication use and concluded they more likely reflect under-diagnosis and under-treatment of Black and Latino children than over-diagnosis of White children [6].

**The picture is not simply "minority children are missed".** A meta-analysis of 21 US studies estimated ADHD at about 14.5% in Black children and adults (13.9% in children alone), higher than the general US estimate, with wide variation between studies [7]. Many included samples were from disadvantaged communities, which may raise the estimate. Together these studies suggest both under-recognition in clinics and real uncertainty about how symptoms are rated and measured.

## What is uncertain or debated

**Bias and access are hard to separate.** The US studies cannot fully tell whether lower diagnosis rates come from less access to care, family reluctance shaped by fear of stigma or discrimination, clinicians interpreting the same behaviour differently, or real differences in rates. The Cénat review's high prevalence estimate and the Shi cohort's lower diagnosis rates measure different things (symptoms in surveys versus diagnoses in insurance claims) [5][7].

**Evidence outside the US is thin.** This search found no comparable large studies of ethnic-minority diagnosis rates in the UK, Australia or Europe. Treat the US findings as a reason to ask questions, not as figures that transfer.

**Gender-diverse people.** A study pooling five datasets (641,860 people, 3,777 gender-diverse) found that transgender and gender-diverse people reported higher rates of several diagnoses, including ADHD, than cisgender people [8]. Diagnoses were self-reported, the strongest associations were with autism and depression, and the study cannot say why. Research on how ADHD assessment works for gender-diverse people is very limited.

**Adults recalling childhood.** Adult diagnosis relies on memory of symptoms before 12, often supported by school reports or a parent's account. Recall is imperfect, which feeds the late-onset debate on [diagnosed as an adult](diagnosed-as-an-adult.md).

## What this means for you

A careful assessment is a structured clinical judgement, not a test score. That can feel less solid than a blood result, but it is how many well-established conditions are diagnosed. If a questionnaire or video has made you wonder, the useful next step is a proper assessment, which also looks for other explanations. Information here is not a diagnosis; see [not medical advice](../08-reading-this-well/not-medical-advice.md).
