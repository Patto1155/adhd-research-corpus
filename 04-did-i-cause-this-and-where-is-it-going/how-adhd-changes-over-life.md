# Will my ADHD get better or worse as I get older?

> **Short answer:** On average, ADHD symptoms ease with age, but most people diagnosed in childhood still have some symptoms as adults. For many, ADHD does not follow a straight line: it comes and goes, with better and worse stretches that often track life demands. How many people "grow out of it" depends heavily on how recovery is defined. Very little research covers people over 65, and whether ADHD can truly begin in adulthood is still debated.

## What the evidence shows

**How many children still have ADHD as adults depends on the definition.** A meta-analysis of follow-up studies found that about 15% of children with ADHD still met the full diagnostic criteria at age 25 [1]. When people who still had some impairing symptoms (partial remission) were included, the figure rose to about 65% [1]. Both numbers come from the same studies; they answer different questions. These studies began with children, so the figures describe adults who were diagnosed young.

**For most people the course fluctuates.** The MTA study followed 558 children with ADHD from about age 10 to about age 25, assessing them eight times [2]. Only 9.1% recovered and stayed recovered, and 10.8% had steady, persistent ADHD throughout. The large majority, 63.8%, moved between periods of remission and recurrence [2]. About 30% reached full remission at some point, but 60% of those saw symptoms return later. About 90% still had some symptoms into young adulthood [2]. The idea that half of children simply outgrow ADHD does not fit these data.

**Adult prevalence falls with age.** A global analysis estimated that 2.58% of adults have ADHD that began in childhood, and 6.76% have adult ADHD symptoms whatever their history [3]. For childhood-onset ADHD, the estimate fell from 5.1% at ages 18–24 to 0.8% over 60 [3]. Methods varied widely between the studies pooled, so these are rough figures. Lower rates in older people may partly reflect how rarely older adults are assessed, not only real decline.

**Older adults are barely studied.** A meta-analysis of ADHD in older adults found <!-- CHECK: the age cut-off the review used for "older adults" (possibly 50+) --> very different figures depending on how it was measured: 2.18% using symptom rating scales, but only 0.23% with a clinical diagnosis [4]. Treated ADHD was rarer again, less than half the rate of diagnosed ADHD [4]. The authors read this as a sign of under-recognition rather than of ADHD disappearing.

**Adult onset is contested.** In New Zealand's Dunedin cohort of 1,037 people followed to age 38, 90% of the 31 adults who met ADHD criteria had no childhood history of it [5]. Of 61 childhood cases, only 3 still met full criteria at 38 [5]. The authors raised the possibility that adult-onset ADHD is a different condition. When the MTA team reassessed apparent late-onset cases carefully, they excluded about 95% of them, mostly because symptoms were better explained by substance use or other conditions, or because there had been milder childhood symptoms all along [6]. <!-- CHECK: the "about 95%" figure is from the scoping report summary of Sibley 2018, not re-seen in search this session --> The usual view now is that most "late" ADHD began in adolescence or was missed, with true adult onset unresolved. More is on [diagnosed as an adult](../01-is-this-real/diagnosed-as-an-adult.md).

**Dementia and life expectancy have their own page.** Studies of ADHD and later dementia, and of life expectancy, are set out with their absolute risks and limits on [the hard statistics in context](the-hard-statistics-in-context.md). In short, links exist in registry data but are much weaker than headlines suggest, and early dementia can be mistaken for late-appearing ADHD.

## What is uncertain or debated

- **Why symptoms fluctuate.** Changes in demands, support, sleep, stress and treatment probably all play a part, but the MTA data cannot say which matters most for a given person. A reanalysis using different definitions found a much smaller fluctuating group, so "most people fluctuate" is debated in degree. <!-- CHECK: scoping report cites a reanalysis giving 17–29% fluctuating; source not identified this session -->
- **Whether symptoms or impairment decline.** Counting symptoms and measuring how much they get in the way of life can give different answers, and studies measure impairment inconsistently. A person can have fewer symptoms on a checklist and still struggle.
- **Self-report versus others' report.** Adults tend to rate their own symptoms differently from how parents or partners rate them, which changes persistence figures.
- **Midlife and menopause.** Many women report symptoms getting worse in their 40s and 50s. Direct research is almost absent; see [hormones, cycles and menopause](hormones-cycles-and-menopause.md).
- **Late life.** There are almost no long-term studies of how ADHD changes after 65, or of how it interacts with normal ageing of memory and attention.

## What this means for you

At group level, ADHD tends to ease with age, and a stretch of good months or years is common. So is a return of difficulties when life changes. These are averages from studies mostly begun in childhood, and they cannot say what your own course will be.
