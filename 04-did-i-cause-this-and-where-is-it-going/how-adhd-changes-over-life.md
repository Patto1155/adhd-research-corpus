# ADHD over a lifetime: will it get better or worse as I age?

> **Short answer:** On average, ADHD symptoms ease with age, but most people diagnosed in childhood still have some symptoms as adults. For many, symptoms come and go, often tracking life demands, and how many "grow out of it" depends on how recovery is defined. People over 65 are barely studied, and whether ADHD can begin in adulthood is debated.

## What the evidence shows

**How many children still have ADHD as adults depends on the definition.** A meta-analysis of follow-up studies found that about 15% of children with ADHD still met the full diagnostic criteria at age 25 [1]. When people in partial remission (still with some impairing symptoms, but below the full threshold) were also counted as persistent, the figure rose to about 65% [1]. Both numbers come from the same studies and answer different questions.

These studies began with children, so the figures describe adults who were diagnosed young.

**For most people the course fluctuates.** The MTA study followed 558 children with ADHD from about age 10 to about age 25, assessing them eight times [2]. Only 9.1% recovered and stayed recovered, and 10.8% had steady, persistent ADHD throughout. The large majority, 63.8%, moved between periods of remission and recurrence [2].

About 30% reached full remission at some point, but 60% of those saw symptoms return later. About 90% still had some symptoms into young adulthood, a figure the study team has reported from the same cohort [2]. The idea that half of children simply outgrow ADHD does not fit these data.

**Adult prevalence falls with age.** A global analysis estimated that 2.58% of adults have ADHD that began in childhood, and 6.76% have adult ADHD symptoms whatever their history [3]. For childhood-onset ADHD, the estimate fell from 5.1% at ages 18–24 to 0.8% over 60 [3].

Methods varied widely between the studies pooled, so these are rough figures. Lower rates in older people may partly reflect how rarely older adults are assessed, not only real decline.

**Older adults are barely studied.** A meta-analysis of ADHD in adults aged 50 and over found very different figures depending on how it was measured: 2.18% using symptom rating scales, but only 0.23% with a clinical diagnosis [4]. Treated ADHD was rarer again, less than half the rate of diagnosed ADHD [4]. The authors read this as a sign of under-recognition rather than of ADHD disappearing.

**Adult onset is contested.** In New Zealand's Dunedin cohort of 1,037 people followed to age 38, 90% of the adults who met ADHD criteria had no childhood history of it [5]. Of 61 childhood cases, only 3 still met full criteria at 38 [5]. The authors raised the possibility that adult-onset ADHD is a different condition.

**Careful reassessment found little true adult onset.** When the MTA team reassessed people who screened positive for late-onset ADHD, about 95% were excluded on careful assessment, most often because the symptoms occurred only during heavy substance use [6]. Most of the remaining late-onset cases had begun in adolescence, and the team found no evidence of adult-onset ADHD without a complex psychiatric history [6].

The usual view now is that most "late" ADHD began in adolescence or was missed, with true adult onset unresolved. More is on [diagnosis as an adult](../01-is-this-real/diagnosed-as-an-adult.md).

**Dementia and life expectancy have their own page.** In short, links exist in registry data but are much weaker than headlines suggest, and early dementia can be mistaken for late-appearing ADHD. The studies, with their absolute risks and limits, are on [Will ADHD shorten my life? The hard statistics in context](the-hard-statistics-in-context.md).

## What is uncertain or debated

- **Why symptoms fluctuate.** Changes in demands, support, sleep, stress and treatment probably all play a part, but the MTA data cannot say which matters most for a given person. How large the fluctuating group looks also depends on how remission is defined.
- **Whether symptoms or impairment decline.** Counting symptoms and measuring how much they get in the way of life can give different answers, and studies measure impairment inconsistently. A person can have fewer symptoms on a checklist and still struggle.
- **Self-report versus others' report.** Adults tend to rate their own symptoms differently from how parents or partners rate them, which changes persistence figures.
- **Midlife and menopause.** Many women report symptoms getting worse in their 40s and 50s. The first studies comparing women with and without ADHD at this stage appeared in 2025 and disagree; see [Do periods, pregnancy and menopause make ADHD worse?](hormones-cycles-and-menopause.md)
- **Late life.** There are almost no long-term studies of how ADHD changes after 65, or of how it interacts with normal ageing of memory and attention.

## What this means for you

At group level, ADHD tends to ease with age, and a stretch of good months or years is common. So is a return of difficulties when life changes. These are averages from studies mostly begun in childhood, and they cannot say what your own course will be.
