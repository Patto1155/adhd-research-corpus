# Do periods, pregnancy and menopause make ADHD worse?

> **Short answer:** Possibly: many women with ADHD report symptoms changing across the menstrual cycle, after giving birth and around menopause, but the research is very thin. The idea is biologically plausible, and the best systematic review found 11 mostly small studies, calling the evidence "largely suggestive". Two 2025 menopause studies disagreed, and almost nothing covers ADHD medicines at different hormonal stages.

## What the evidence shows

**The evidence base is small, stage by stage.** This page is short on findings because the field is. To be specific about the gap:

- **The menstrual cycle and puberty:** 11 studies in the most thorough review to date, mostly small [1].
- **After giving birth:** two surveys of women with ADHD, neither with a comparison group [2, 3].
- **Perimenopause and menopause:** a handful of studies, all surveys rather than studies that follow women through the transition. The two largest, both published in 2025, compared women with and without ADHD and did not agree with each other [4, 5].
- **Medication and hormones:** no trials of ADHD medicines in women with ADHD at menopause were found for this page.

**Online claims mostly rest on experience.** Much of what circulates online about hormones and ADHD comes from clinical experience and personal accounts. Those are worth taking seriously as starting points for research. They are not yet evidence about how common the effect is or who it affects.

**There is a plausible mechanism.** Oestrogen and progesterone interact with the brain's dopamine pathways, which ADHD medicines also act on, which is why researchers expect hormonal changes to affect symptoms [6]. The evidence for this comes mostly from animal and general-population studies, not from people with ADHD.

**Cycle and puberty findings point the same way, weakly.** A 2025 systematic review of 11 studies concluded that evidence was "largely suggestive" of links between sex hormones and ADHD symptoms in females, mainly in puberty and across the menstrual cycle [1]. The studies were few and often small, and the authors called for research on other life stages, including menopause [1]. "Suggestive" here means consistent with a link but not enough to say how strong it is or who it affects.

**Tests of thinking may dip before a period.** A narrative review of 29 studies found that four small studies in women with ADHD reported weaker attention, executive function and impulse control in the second half of the cycle, before a period [7]. In women without ADHD, attention tended to be *better* at that phase [7]. The authors called the findings preliminary and sometimes contradictory, because the studies were small and used different methods [7].

**Mood symptoms around hormonal changes were common in one survey.** In a survey of 209 women with ADHD aged 18 to 71, recruited from a Dutch clinic, about 45% (95 women) reported symptoms suggestive of premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD, a severe form of premenstrual mood change) [2]. Among those who had given birth, 57.6% scored above a common screening cut-off for postnatal depression [2]. Women in the menopause transition reported more anxiety, low mood, sexual difficulties, hot flushes and physical complaints [2].

**Those survey figures may overestimate.** The study had no comparison group of women without ADHD, PMDD symptoms were recalled rather than tracked day by day, and postnatal symptoms were asked about years later [2]. The 45% and 57.6% figures come from secondary summaries of the paper; its abstract gives no percentages [2].

**A larger 2026 survey found a gap between recalled and measured symptoms.** In an online survey of 600 women who reported an ADHD diagnosis, 70% said their symptoms had worsened after giving birth, 98% said so around menopause, and 89% of those still having periods (and not on hormonal treatment) noticed changes across the cycle, mostly worse before a period [3]. Yet when current symptoms were scored with a standard ADHD questionnaire, severity was similar across life stages [3]. Self-selected online samples and recalled change can both inflate figures like these, so the two results are best read together [3].

If low mood after a birth, or at any other time, is weighing on you now, there are contacts on [where to get help now](../08-reading-this-well/not-medical-advice.md#if-you-need-help-now).

**An Icelandic study found more perimenopausal symptoms with ADHD.** In a population study of 5,392 women aged 35 to 55, the 535 who reported an ADHD diagnosis had higher perimenopausal symptom scores than the rest (18 against 13), and the gap was largest at ages 35 to 39, suggesting symptoms may start earlier [4]. Severe symptoms were reported by 54% of women with ADHD against 30% without: about 1.8 times as common, or 24 more women in every 100 [4]. The diagnosis was self-reported rather than checked against records, and all symptoms were self-reported.

**A UK survey found the opposite on the main question.** In 656 women aged 45 to 60, the 245 with an existing ADHD diagnosis did not report worse menopausal symptoms than women without one, at any menopausal stage [5]. Across the whole sample, though, women with more ADHD traits did report more menopausal complaints, including anxiety, low mood, memory and concentration, and these links were weaker in women with a diagnosis [5]. The survey excluded women using menopausal hormone therapy or the contraceptive pill, and about nine in ten participants were White [5].

**Neither menopause study followed women through the transition.** So neither shows whether menopause makes ADHD itself worse.

**An expert group says hormonal transitions matter and treatment research is missing.** A 2025 review by a European special interest group on female ADHD concluded that hormonal transitions worsen ADHD symptoms and mood, and that pharmacological research and tailored treatments are lacking [6]. This is an expert synthesis of a thin evidence base, drawing partly on women's own accounts, not a new study. Its confident wording goes further than the systematic review [1] and the disagreeing menopause studies [4, 5] support.

**The menopause medication trial was not in people with ADHD.** A small 2015 crossover trial gave lisdexamfetamine, an ADHD stimulant, and a placebo to 32 healthy women around menopause *without* ADHD who had new problems with planning and memory [8]. Over four weeks, self-rated executive function and one memory test improved, other thinking tests did not, and blood pressure and heart rate rose [8]. This says nothing directly about women who already have ADHD.

## What is uncertain or debated

- **How much hormones change ADHD.** How often symptoms change with the cycle, by how much, and whether menopause makes ADHD itself worse or adds separate symptoms (poor sleep, low mood, "brain fog") that look similar are all open.
- **Overlap with menopause symptoms.** Concentration and memory complaints are common in menopause in women without ADHD. Studies would need to separate the two, and none has yet done so convincingly.
- **Hormonal contraception and hormone therapy.** Their effects on ADHD symptoms have not been studied well enough to summarise here.
- **Who is studied.** Women in midlife have rarely been studied, and the UK menopause survey excluded women using hormone therapy or the pill [5]. Trans and gender-diverse people are almost absent from this research.

## What this means for you

If your symptoms seem to shift with your cycle, after pregnancy or in midlife, that experience is widely reported and fits what little research exists. The evidence cannot yet say how common it is or what helps, so claims that sound precise are ahead of the science. Any change to treatment around these stages is a decision to make with a prescriber.
