# Do periods, pregnancy and menopause make ADHD worse?

> **Short answer:** Possibly: many women with ADHD report symptoms changing across the menstrual cycle, after giving birth and around menopause, but the research is very thin. The idea is biologically plausible, and the best systematic review found 11 mostly small studies, calling the evidence "largely suggestive". Two 2025 menopause surveys disagreed, and almost nothing covers ADHD medicines at different hormonal stages.

## What the evidence shows

**The evidence base is small, stage by stage.** This page is short on findings because the field is. To be specific about the gap:

- **The menstrual cycle and puberty:** 11 studies in the most thorough review to date, mostly small [1].
- **After giving birth:** one survey of 209 women with ADHD, with no comparison group [2].
- **Perimenopause and menopause:** a handful of studies, all surveys rather than studies that follow women through the transition. The two largest, both published in 2025, compared women with and without ADHD and did not agree with each other [4, 6].
- **Medication and hormones:** no trials of ADHD medicines in women with ADHD at menopause were found for this page.

**Online claims mostly rest on experience.** Much of what circulates online about hormones and ADHD comes from clinical experience and personal accounts. Those are worth taking seriously as starting points for research. They are not yet evidence about how common the effect is or who it affects.

**There is a plausible mechanism.** Oestrogen and progesterone interact with the brain's dopamine pathways, which ADHD medicines also act on, which is why researchers expect hormonal changes to affect symptoms [3]. The evidence for this comes mostly from animal and general-population studies, not from people with ADHD.

**Cycle and puberty findings point the same way, weakly.** A 2025 systematic review of 11 studies concluded that evidence was "largely suggestive" of links between sex hormones and ADHD symptoms in females, mainly in puberty and across the menstrual cycle [1]. The studies were few and often small, and the authors called for research on other life stages, including menopause [1]. "Suggestive" here means consistent with a link but not enough to say how strong it is or who it affects.

**Mood symptoms around hormonal changes were common in one survey.** In a survey of 209 women with ADHD aged 18 to 71, recruited from a Dutch clinic, about 45% (95 women) reported symptoms suggestive of premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD, a severe form of premenstrual mood change) [2]. Among those who had given birth, 57.6% scored above a common screening cut-off for postnatal depression [2]. Women in the menopause transition reported more anxiety, low mood, sexual difficulties, hot flushes and physical complaints [2].

**Those survey figures may overestimate.** The study had no comparison group of women without ADHD, PMDD symptoms were recalled rather than tracked day by day, and postnatal symptoms were asked about years later [2].

**An Icelandic study found more perimenopausal symptoms with ADHD.** In a population study of about 5,400 women, the 535 who screened positive for ADHD symptoms reported more perimenopausal symptoms than the rest (an average score of 18 against 13), and the gap was largest in women aged 35 to 39, suggesting symptoms may start earlier [4]. ADHD was measured with a short six-question screener rather than a diagnosis, and everything was self-reported.

**A UK survey found the opposite on the main question.** In 656 women aged 45 to 60, those with a formal ADHD diagnosis did not report worse menopausal symptoms than women without one [6]. Across the whole sample, though, women with more ADHD traits did report more menopausal complaints, especially with sleep, concentration and mood [6]. The UK survey excluded women using hormone therapy.

**Neither menopause study followed women through the transition.** So neither shows whether menopause makes ADHD itself worse.

**An expert group says hormonal transitions matter and treatment research is missing.** A 2025 review by a European special interest group on female ADHD concluded that hormonal transitions tend to worsen ADHD symptoms and mood, and that pharmacological research and tailored treatments are lacking [3]. This is an expert synthesis of a thin evidence base, not a new study.

**The menopause medication trial was not in people with ADHD.** A small 2015 trial gave lisdexamfetamine, an ADHD stimulant, to 32 women aged 45 to 60 *without* ADHD who had new problems with planning and memory at menopause [5]. Over four weeks, self-rated executive function and one memory test improved, and blood pressure and heart rate rose [5]. This says nothing directly about women who already have ADHD.

## What is uncertain or debated

- **How much hormones change ADHD.** How often symptoms change with the cycle, by how much, and whether menopause makes ADHD itself worse or adds separate symptoms (poor sleep, low mood, "brain fog") that look similar are all open.
- **Overlap with menopause symptoms.** Concentration and memory complaints are common in menopause in women without ADHD. Studies would need to separate the two, and none has yet done so convincingly.
- **Hormonal contraception and hormone therapy.** Their effects on ADHD symptoms have not been studied well enough to summarise here.
- **Who is studied.** Women in midlife have rarely been studied, and the UK menopause survey excluded women using hormone therapy [6]. Trans and gender-diverse people are almost absent from this research.

## What this means for you

If your symptoms seem to shift with your cycle, after pregnancy or in midlife, that experience is widely reported and fits what little research exists. The evidence cannot yet say how common it is or what helps, so claims that sound precise are ahead of the science. Any change to treatment around these stages is a decision to make with a prescriber.
