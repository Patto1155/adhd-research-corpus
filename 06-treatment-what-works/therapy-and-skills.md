# Do therapy, coaching and skills training work for ADHD?

> **Short answer:** Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) adapted for ADHD has the best evidence among talking treatments for adults, with moderate-certainty evidence that it reduces symptoms compared with being on a waiting list. Its advantage over other active therapies is less clear, and in a 2025 analysis it beat control conditions only when clinicians did the rating. For children, parent training reliably improves parenting and behaviour problems even where its effect on core symptoms disappears under blinded rating. Coaching and psychoeducation have thin evidence.

## What the evidence shows

**CBT in adults: moderate evidence against waiting lists.** A Cochrane review of 14 trials and about 700 adults found CBT reduced self-reported ADHD symptoms compared with a waiting list (SMD 0.84, moderate certainty) [1]. Compared with other active therapies such as supportive therapy, there was no clear advantage (low certainty) [1]. The 2025 BMJ umbrella review rated adult CBT among the few ADHD treatments with at least moderate-certainty evidence [2]. CBT for ADHD usually focuses on practical skills such as planning, organisation and managing procrastination, alongside unhelpful thinking patterns.

**But the rater matters.** A 2025 analysis of 113 adult trials found that CBT (SMD about 0.76), mindfulness (0.79) and psychoeducation (0.77) beat control conditions only on clinician ratings, not on adults' own ratings; only stimulants and atomoxetine improved both [3]. Clinicians in these trials usually knew who had received therapy. This does not mean the therapies do nothing. It does mean part of the measured effect may reflect expectation.

**For children, behavioural parent training changes behaviour and parenting.** In children, behavioural treatment shows no clear effect on core ADHD symptoms when raters are probably blind to who received it [4]. A meta-analysis by the European ADHD Guidelines Group found that, under probably blinded assessment, it still improved positive parenting (SMD 0.63), reduced negative parenting (0.43) and reduced conduct problems (0.31) [5]. <!-- CHECK: blinded conduct-problems SMD 0.31 came from the accepted manuscript; the published version may differ after three trials were added --> More on this is in [parenting and what helps](../07-for-parents-and-partners/parenting-and-what-helps.md).

**Computer-based "brain training" improves the trained task, not daily symptoms.** A meta-analysis of 16 trials (759 children) found cognitive training improved working-memory test scores. Effects on ADHD symptoms fell from an SMD of 0.37 with unblinded raters to 0.20 with probably blinded raters, and the inattention effect was no longer clear [6]. A 2023 update found no clear effect on blinded total symptom ratings. <!-- CHECK: 2023 Molecular Psychiatry update (SMD 0.12, probably blinded) seen in search; full citation not captured -->

**Coaching and psychoeducation: thin evidence.** A systematic review of psychological treatments for adults found behavioural approaches helped inattention but no single type of therapy was conclusively superior; it found no clear effect of psychoeducation or cognitive training against controls [7]. <!-- CHECK: Scholz review journal not captured --> Controlled trials of ADHD coaching are few and small. ## What is uncertain or debated

Blinding is the central problem for every talking or skills treatment. People know whether they are having therapy, and so do the clinicians who rate them, so expectation can inflate results. Waiting-list comparisons are especially generous, because people on a waiting list may do worse simply from being told to wait.

Most adult trials are short and small, and long-term benefit has not been shown with high certainty for any ADHD treatment [2]. Trials often enrol people already on stable medication, so results describe CBT added to medication more than CBT alone. Very little is known about which person benefits from which approach. No trial evidence was found on whether therapy works differently for people diagnosed late in life.

## What this means for you

Therapy and skills training aim at different things from medication: habits, systems, relationships and how someone thinks about their difficulties. The evidence supports CBT as a reasonable option with modest, moderately certain benefits, and parent training for children's behaviour and family stress. Needing medication, therapy, both or neither is not a measure of effort or character; which mix suits a person is something to discuss with a clinician.
