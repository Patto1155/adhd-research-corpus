# Therapy, coaching and skills training: do they work for ADHD?

> **Short answer:** Some do, modestly: CBT adapted for ADHD has the best evidence among talking treatments for adults, with moderate-certainty, short-term benefit against waiting lists, but in a 2025 analysis it beat controls only on clinician ratings. Parent training improves parenting and children's behaviour, not core ADHD symptoms on blinded ratings. Coaching and psychoeducation have thin evidence.

## What the evidence shows

**CBT in adults: moderate evidence against waiting lists.** A Cochrane review of 14 trials and about 700 adults found cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) reduced self-reported ADHD symptoms compared with a waiting list, with a standardised mean difference (SMD, how far apart two group averages are, in units of how spread out people's scores are; by convention 0.8 is large) of 0.84, from 5 trials and 251 people, at moderate certainty [1]. The authors judged the benefit to be short term. Compared with other active therapies such as supportive therapy, there was no clear advantage (low certainty) [1].

The 2025 BMJ umbrella review rated adult CBT among the few ADHD treatments with at least moderate-certainty evidence [2]. CBT for ADHD usually focuses on practical skills such as planning, organisation and managing procrastination, alongside unhelpful thinking patterns.

**But who does the rating matters.** A 2025 analysis of 113 adult trials found that CBT (SMD about 0.76), mindfulness (0.79) and psychoeducation (0.77) beat control conditions only on clinician ratings, not on adults' own ratings; only stimulants and atomoxetine improved both [3]. Clinicians in these trials usually knew who had received therapy. This does not mean the therapies do nothing, but it does mean part of the measured effect may reflect expectation.

**For children, parent training changes behaviour and parenting.** In children, behavioural treatment shows no clear effect on core ADHD symptoms when raters are probably blind to who received it [4]. A meta-analysis by the European ADHD Guidelines Group found that, under probably blinded assessment, it still improved positive parenting (SMD 0.63), reduced negative parenting (0.43) and reduced conduct problems (0.31) [5]. More on this is in [parenting and what helps](../07-family-work-and-other-people/parenting-and-what-helps.md).

**Computer-based "brain training" improves the trained task, not daily symptoms.** A meta-analysis of 16 trials (759 children) found cognitive training improved working-memory test scores. Effects on ADHD symptoms fell from an SMD of 0.37 with unblinded raters to 0.20 with probably blinded raters, and the inattention effect was no longer clear [6]. A 2023 update of 36 trials found no clear effect on probably blinded ratings of total symptoms (SMD 0.12), with clinical effects limited to small, short-term effects on inattention, although working memory test scores improved [8].

**Coaching and psychoeducation: thin evidence.** A systematic review of psychological treatments for adults found behavioural approaches helped inattention but no single type of therapy was conclusively superior; it found no clear effect of psychoeducation, cognitive training or neurofeedback against controls [7]. Controlled trials of ADHD coaching are few and small. No high-quality review of coaching was located for this page.

## What is uncertain or debated

**Blinding is the central problem for every talking or skills treatment.** People know whether they are having therapy, and so do the clinicians who rate them, so expectation can inflate results. Waiting-list comparisons are especially generous, because people on a waiting list may do worse simply from being told to wait.

**Trials are short, small and often add therapy to medication.** Most adult trials are short and small, and long-term benefit has not been shown with high certainty for any ADHD treatment [2]. Trials often enrol people already on stable medication, so results describe CBT added to medication more than CBT alone.

**Who benefits is unknown.** Very little is known about which person benefits from which approach. No trial evidence was found on whether therapy works differently for people diagnosed late in life.

## What this means for you

Therapy and skills training aim at different things from medication: habits, systems, relationships and how someone thinks about their difficulties. The evidence supports CBT as a reasonable option with modest, moderately certain benefits, and parent training for children's behaviour and family stress. Needing medication, therapy, both or neither is not a measure of effort or character; which mix suits a person is something to discuss with a clinician.
