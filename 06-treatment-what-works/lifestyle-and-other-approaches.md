# Neurofeedback, diet, supplements, exercise: do they help ADHD?

> **Short answer:** Mostly not on blinded measures: most look helpful when raters know who received them, and the benefit shrinks or disappears when raters do not. Neurofeedback shows essentially no blinded effect; omega-3 and restriction diets show small effects at best, mostly in children. Exercise shows a small effect in the strictest analysis, and mindfulness larger effects only at low certainty.

## Why blinding is the key question

**The rater can create the result.** In a drug trial, a placebo pill hides who is getting the real treatment. For neurofeedback, special diets or exercise programs, the participant and their family nearly always know. If parents have invested time and money in a treatment, their ratings tend to improve whatever the treatment does.

**Researchers therefore split the ratings.** A landmark 2013 meta-analysis compared ratings from people "most proximal" to treatment (usually parents, unblinded) with ratings from "probably blinded" assessors, such as teachers unaware of the treatment or observers [1]. With unblinded ratings, every dietary and psychological treatment looked effective.

With probably blinded ratings, only free fatty acid supplements (standardised mean difference, SMD, 0.16) and removal of artificial food colours (SMD 0.42, in children selected for suspected food sensitivity) remained statistically significant [1]. An SMD measures how far apart two group averages are, in units of how spread out people's scores are; by convention 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large, so 0.16 is very small. These findings are in children.

## What the evidence shows

**Neurofeedback: no meaningful effect when raters are blinded.** Neurofeedback trains people to change their own brain-wave patterns using real-time feedback from scalp sensors. A 2025 meta-analysis of 38 randomised trials (2,472 participants) found probably blinded ratings of ADHD symptoms showed no improvement (SMD 0.04, effectively zero; 20 trials) [2]. A small effect (SMD 0.21) appeared only in nine trials using well-established standard protocols [2].

The authors concluded that trials with probably blinded outcomes do not support neurofeedback as a stand-alone treatment for ADHD [2]. An earlier 2016 meta-analysis of 13 trials reached the same overall conclusion: effects were not significant on probably blinded ratings or against active or sham controls [3]. Neurofeedback is often expensive and time-consuming, so this result is directly relevant to readers weighing it.

**A claim of lasting effects rests on unblinded ratings.** A 2019 meta-analysis of 10 trials with follow-up of 2 to 12 months found parent-rated benefits of neurofeedback held or grew (inattention SMD 0.64 after treatment and 0.80 at follow-up, within the neurofeedback groups), and beat inactive comparison groups at follow-up (SMD 0.57) [4]. Those were parent ratings, mostly against inactive controls, and several authors work in or with the neurofeedback field; active treatments, mainly methylphenidate, still did better straight after treatment [4]. It does not test what blinded raters see, so it does not overturn the blinded result above.

**Omega-3 and other fatty acids: small or no effect.** An older meta-analysis of 10 trials (699 children) found omega-3 supplements had a small effect on children's ADHD symptoms (SMD 0.31), with higher doses of one fatty acid, EPA, linked to larger effects [5]. A 2023 Cochrane review of 37 trials found high-certainty evidence that fatty-acid supplements made no difference to parent-rated total symptoms compared with placebo over the medium term (SMD −0.08, 16 trials) [6]. A smaller, low-certainty analysis of three trials suggested some children may respond [6].

**Restriction diets and food colours: small effects in selected children.** Restriction diets (removing many foods, then reintroducing them) showed an average effect of about 0.29 in children [7]. For synthetic food colours, the parent-rated effect was small (about 0.18) and shrank to about 0.12 after adjusting for publication bias (the tendency for positive studies to be published more often); it was not reliable when teachers or observers did the rating [7].

The authors estimated that around 8% of children with ADHD may have symptoms related to synthetic food colours; that is an estimate for a subgroup of children with ADHD, not for children in general [7]. Sugar as a cause is covered on [what does and doesn't cause ADHD](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/what-does-and-doesnt-cause-adhd.md).

**Exercise: few and small trials, mostly in children.** In children, a 2015 meta-analysis of a small number of small trials reported moderate to large effects of aerobic exercise on attention, hyperactivity and impulsivity, and on executive function [8].

A stricter 2022 meta-analysis, limited to randomised trials in children with a clinical diagnosis and symptoms measured on validated rating scales, found only a small effect of regular moderate-to-vigorous activity on core symptoms (Hedges' g 0.33, a version of the SMD; 11 trials), with a range that came close to zero [9]. Executive-function tests are not the same as ADHD symptoms, and symptom ratings in exercise trials are rarely blinded.

In adults, a 2024 systematic review, published as a conference abstract, found single bouts of exercise may have a small positive effect on thinking skills, with low certainty of evidence [10]. Exercise has other well-established health benefits; the question here is only whether it treats ADHD.

**Mindfulness: small to moderate effects, at low certainty.** In a meta-analysis of 10 small studies in children and adults, mindfulness had small-to-moderate effects, most clearly on inattention [11]. The BMJ umbrella review found large effects of mindfulness in adults, but with low certainty [12]. The same review found large effects for acupuncture and CBT in children and adolescents, also at low certainty [12]; why these were rated low, for example because of few or unblinded trials, was not checked for this page.

## What is uncertain or debated

- **Whether benefits last.** Follow-up data exist mainly for neurofeedback and rely on parent ratings [4]; whether blinded raters see lasting change has not been shown.
- **Trial quality.** Many of these trials are small, short and run by groups who developed or promote the treatment.
- **Adults.** Adult data are scarce for almost everything on this page apart from neurofeedback and mindfulness.
- **What a gap between raters means.** Blinded raters such as teachers see children in a different setting from parents, so a gap between ratings could sometimes reflect a real effect at home that teachers miss. This possibility is debated but untested in most trials.
- **Other benefits.** A treatment that improves sleep, fitness or mood can still be worth having for those reasons even if it does not reduce ADHD symptoms.

## What this means for you

When a product or program reports dramatic improvement, the useful question is who did the rating and whether they knew. For most approaches on this page, effects largely disappear when that is controlled. This page reports evidence and does not recommend buying or avoiding any product; how any approach fits with other treatment is worth discussing with a clinician.
