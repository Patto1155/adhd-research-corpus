# Do neurofeedback, diet, supplements or exercise help ADHD?

> **Short answer:** Most of these approaches look helpful when the people rating the results know who received them, and most of the apparent benefit shrinks or disappears when raters do not know. Neurofeedback shows essentially no effect on blinded symptom ratings. Omega-3 supplements and restriction diets show small effects at best, mostly in children. Exercise has modest, mostly short-term effects on attention and thinking, mainly in children. None has evidence comparable to medication or CBT.

## Why blinding is the key question

**The rater can create the result.** In a drug trial, a placebo pill hides who is getting the real treatment. For neurofeedback, special diets or exercise programs, the participant and their family nearly always know. If parents have invested time and money in a treatment, their ratings tend to improve whatever the treatment does.

**Researchers therefore split the ratings.** A landmark 2013 meta-analysis compared ratings from people "most proximal" to treatment (usually parents, unblinded) with ratings from "probably blinded" assessors, such as teachers unaware of the treatment or observers [1]. With unblinded ratings, every dietary and psychological treatment looked effective. With probably blinded ratings, only free fatty acid supplements (SMD 0.16, a very small effect) and removal of artificial food colours (SMD 0.42, in children selected for suspected food sensitivity) remained statistically significant [1]. These findings are in children.

## What the evidence shows

**Neurofeedback: no meaningful effect when raters are blinded.** Neurofeedback trains people to change their own brain-wave patterns using real-time feedback from scalp sensors. A 2025 meta-analysis of 38 randomised trials (2,472 participants aged 5 to 40) found probably blinded ratings of ADHD symptoms showed no improvement (SMD 0.04, effectively zero; 20 trials) [2]. A small effect (SMD 0.21) appeared only in nine trials using standard protocols, and the only test-based gain was a small improvement in processing speed [2]. An earlier 2016 meta-analysis reached the same overall conclusion [3]. Neurofeedback is often expensive and time-consuming, so this result is directly relevant to readers weighing it.

**Omega-3 and other fatty acids: small or none.** An older meta-analysis found omega-3 supplements had a small effect on children's ADHD symptoms (SMD about 0.3), with formulations higher in one fatty acid, EPA, doing better [4]. A 2023 Cochrane review of 37 trials found high-certainty evidence that fatty-acid supplements made no difference to parent-rated total symptoms compared with placebo over the medium term [5].

**Restriction diets and food colours: small effects in selected children.** Restriction diets (removing many foods, then reintroducing them) showed an average effect of about 0.29 in children, and removing synthetic food colours about 0.21; the food-colour effect did not survive correction for publication bias (the tendency for positive studies to be published more often) [6]. The authors estimated around 8% of children with ADHD might react to food colours [6]. <!-- CHECK: confirm whether Nigg 2012's ~8% estimate refers to children with ADHD or children in general --> Sugar as a cause is covered on [what does and doesn't cause ADHD](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/what-does-and-doesnt-cause-adhd.md).

**Exercise: modest, short-term, mostly in children.** In children, exercise programs showed moderate effects on attention and executive function in small, varied trials [7]. <!-- CHECK: SMD about 0.45–0.48 (PLoS One 2023 meta-analysis, PMC10434964) is from the scoping report; authors not captured --> In adults, a 2024 systematic review found single bouts of exercise may slightly improve thinking skills, with low or very low certainty for every outcome [8]. <!-- CHECK: Hanbury-Brown et al. 2024; journal and full author list not captured --> Exercise has other well-established health benefits; the question here is only whether it treats ADHD.

**Mindfulness: small to moderate, larger against weak comparisons.** In a meta-analysis, mindfulness had small-to-moderate effects, larger against inactive controls and weak against active ones [9]. The BMJ umbrella review found large effects of mindfulness in adults, but with low certainty [10]. The same review found large effects for acupuncture in children, also at low certainty [10].

## What is uncertain or debated

Many of these trials are small, short and run by groups who developed or promote the treatment. Adult data are scarce for almost everything on this page apart from neurofeedback and mindfulness. Blinded raters such as teachers see children in a different setting from parents, so a gap between ratings could sometimes reflect a real effect at home that teachers miss; this possibility is debated but untested in most trials. A treatment that improves sleep, fitness or mood can still be worth having for those reasons even if it does not reduce ADHD symptoms.

## What this means for you

When a product or program reports dramatic improvement, the useful question is who did the rating and whether they knew. For most approaches on this page, effects largely disappear when that is controlled. This page reports evidence and does not recommend buying or avoiding any product; how any approach fits with other treatment is worth discussing with a clinician.
