# If I stop medication, do the benefits go away? Do I have to take it forever?

> **Short answer:** ADHD medicines work while they are being taken; they are not a course that cures. Trials that switch long-term users to placebo without telling them find that symptoms come back for many people, but not for everyone: in one trial in children treated for over two years, most did not clearly worsen. Guidelines therefore recommend regular review of whether treatment is still needed. There is no high-certainty evidence about benefit beyond about a year, so neither "you must take it forever" nor "it stops working" is supported.

## What the evidence shows

**How these trials work.** In a randomised discontinuation trial (also called a randomised withdrawal trial), people already doing well on a medicine are randomly assigned either to keep taking it or to switch to an identical-looking placebo, without knowing which. Researchers then count how many in each group worsen. These trials usually enrol people who have already responded and tolerated the drug, which is called an enriched design.

**Children after more than two years of treatment.** A Dutch trial randomised 94 children and adolescents aged 8 to 18 who had taken methylphenidate in ordinary care for more than two years [1]. Over seven weeks (a three-week taper then four weeks on placebo), clinicians rated 40.4% of those switched to placebo as worse, compared with 15.9% of those who continued. The authors concluded that methylphenidate was still effective on average. They also noted that most children who stopped did not clearly worsen, which supports periodic reassessment.

**Adults: a large difference in one trial.** In an adult trial, people who had been stable on lisdexamfetamine for at least six months were randomised to continue or switch to placebo. Symptoms relapsed in 8.9% of those who continued and 75.0% of those on placebo [2]. Relapse was defined as a 50% or greater rise in symptom score plus worsening on a clinician rating. A similar design in children and adolescents also found more treatment failure after switching to placebo [3].

**Quality of life after stopping.** A meta-analysis of five withdrawal trials (1,463 people) found that switching to placebo was associated with a small drop in quality of life in children and adolescents (SMD 0.19), but not in adults [4].

**What happened in the long-term MTA follow-up.** The MTA study randomised 579 children to different treatments for 14 months, then followed them for years while families chose their own treatment. By eight years, the original groups no longer differed, and medication use in the previous year was not linked to better functioning [5]. At 16 years, extended medication use was associated with shorter adult height but not with fewer adult symptoms [6]. Because families chose whether to continue, those who stayed on medication may have had more severe ADHD, so this does not show medication stops working.

**What guidelines say.** NICE in England's quality standard says that people with ADHD who are taking medication should have a specialist review at least once a year to assess whether they still need it, drawing on recommendation 1.10.1 of the NICE guideline [7]. The review is done by an ADHD specialist or, if the person and their specialist agree, in primary care under a shared-care arrangement. It covers the person's preferences, how well treatment is working across the day, side effects, whether the medicine and dose have been optimised, and effects on education or work [7]. ## What is uncertain or debated

**Long-term evidence is thin for everyone.** A 2025 umbrella review found no high-certainty evidence of long-term benefit for any ADHD treatment, medicine or otherwise [8]. That is a statement about missing evidence, not evidence of no benefit.

**Withdrawal trials may overstate relapse.** People who have already responded are the ones enrolled, and a short-term rebound after stopping can be mistaken for the condition returning. Equally, side effects that disappear on placebo can tell some people which group they are in. These are general methodological points about the design rather than findings from a specific study.

**Few adult trials, and mostly industry-run.** Adult withdrawal data are limited to a few drugs and usually run for weeks, not years. ADHD symptoms also fluctuate naturally over time (see [how ADHD changes over life](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/how-adhd-changes-over-life.md)), which makes it hard to know whether someone would have improved anyway.

**No good trials of "medication holidays".** Planned breaks, such as over school holidays, are common practice; robust evidence on their benefits and harms was not found for this page.

## What this means for you

The evidence suggests ADHD medication keeps working for many people who take it long term, and that some people may not need it indefinitely; it does not tell you which group you are in. That is the purpose of regular review. Starting, pausing, reducing or stopping medication are decisions to make with your prescriber, not on the basis of this page.
