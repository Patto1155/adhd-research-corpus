# How do ADHD medicines actually work?

> **Short answer:** Most ADHD medicines increase the signalling of two brain chemical messengers, dopamine and noradrenaline, mainly in circuits linking the front of the brain to deeper structures that handle attention, motivation and self-control. Stimulants (methylphenidate and the amphetamines) do this quickly and wear off within hours. Non-stimulants work more slowly or act on a different part of the same system. How this chemistry turns into better day-to-day attention is still only partly understood, and a good response to a stimulant does not prove that someone has ADHD.

## What the evidence shows

**Stimulants raise dopamine and noradrenaline between nerve cells.** Nerve cells release these messengers and then pull them back in through "transporter" proteins. Methylphenidate blocks the dopamine transporter (DAT) and the noradrenaline transporter (NET), so the messengers stay active for longer. Amphetamines block the same transporters and also push extra dopamine and noradrenaline out of the nerve cell [1][2]. <!-- CHECK: transporter pharmacology summary is standard but taken from scoping M15, which marks the pharmacology as RECALLED; confirm against Arnsten & Pliszka 2011 or Del Campo 2011 text -->

**Non-stimulants act more narrowly.** Atomoxetine and viloxazine mainly block the noradrenaline transporter. Guanfacine and clonidine work differently: they stimulate a receptor called the alpha-2A adrenoceptor, which in the prefrontal cortex is thought to strengthen the signal that working-memory circuits hold on to [1]. <!-- CHECK: viloxazine mechanism (also described as serotonin-modulating) not confirmed this session; search budget ran out before it could be checked --> All of these drugs outperformed placebo for symptoms in short-term trials in children, and all except modafinil did in adults [3]. Modafinil is not licensed for ADHD in most countries. <!-- CHECK: licensing statement is from memory -->

**There is a "sweet spot", not a "more is better" curve.** Animal and human work on the prefrontal cortex shows an inverted-U relationship: moderate levels of dopamine and noradrenaline signalling improve focus and working memory, while levels that are too low or too high impair them [1]. This is one reason dose-finding with a prescriber matters and why more medicine does not always mean more benefit. Most of this mechanism comes from animal studies, so it is strong as an account of how the drugs act and weaker as an account of what causes ADHD.

**It is not only about dopamine.** Reviews of brain imaging and pharmacology describe both dopamine and noradrenaline modulating networks that run between the frontal lobes, the striatum (a deep structure involved in reward and action) and the cerebellum. The authors state plainly that the precise mechanisms of benefit "remain to be fully specified" [2]. The page on [dopamine, noradrenaline and myths](../02-whats-happening-in-my-brain/dopamine-noradrenaline-and-myths.md) explains why "ADHD is low dopamine" is not an established fact.

**Stimulants affect people without ADHD too.** In a small 1978 placebo-controlled study, a single dose of dextroamphetamine changed attention and activity in boys without ADHD in a similar direction to boys with ADHD [4]. <!-- CHECK: the 1978 abstract itself was not read; the "similar effect" description comes from a later paper citing it (Rapoport et al. caffeine study, Am J Psychiatry 1981). Confirm the finding and the sample size --> So a good response to medication is not a diagnostic test, and a poor response does not rule ADHD out.

**Speed of action differs by drug class.** Stimulants work on the day they are taken and wear off over hours, depending on the formulation. Atomoxetine and the alpha-2A drugs usually take several weeks to reach their full effect. <!-- CHECK: onset-time statements are standard prescribing knowledge from memory; cite a guideline (NICE NG87 or AADPA 2022) if kept --> This is why short trials of non-stimulants can underestimate them, and why stimulants can be taken on some days and not others in a way non-stimulants usually are not.

## What is uncertain or debated

The link between the chemistry and the everyday effect is the weak point. Researchers can show where the drugs bind and what they do to messenger levels. They cannot yet explain why one person's attention improves sharply while another's barely changes, or predict in advance who will respond to which drug.

There is no test of "dopamine level" that could guide choice of medicine. Brain imaging studies of the dopamine transporter in ADHD point in inconsistent directions, and some of that inconsistency may reflect earlier medication exposure [2]. Whether long-term stimulant use changes the brain's own transporter levels is debated and has not been settled.

Most of the detailed mechanism comes from rodents and monkeys, and from single doses given in laboratories. How it applies to years of daily use in adults is less well studied.

## What this means for you

Medicines for ADHD are not a crude sedative or a "speed" that forces focus. They adjust signalling in specific brain systems, and their effect depends on getting the amount right for the individual. Which medicine is used, and how much, is a decision for you and a prescriber; this page explains the mechanism and does not recommend any option.
