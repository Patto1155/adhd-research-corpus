# How do ADHD medicines work?

> **Short answer:** Most ADHD medicines increase signalling by one or both of two brain messengers, dopamine and noradrenaline, in circuits that handle attention, motivation and self-control. Stimulants act quickly and wear off within hours; non-stimulants work more slowly or act differently. How this becomes better everyday attention is only partly understood, and responding to a stimulant does not prove ADHD.

## What the evidence shows

**Stimulants raise dopamine and noradrenaline between nerve cells.** Nerve cells release these messengers and then pull them back in through "transporter" proteins. Methylphenidate blocks the dopamine transporter (DAT) and the noradrenaline transporter (NET), so the messengers stay active for longer. Amphetamines block the same transporters and also push extra dopamine and noradrenaline out of the nerve cell [1, 2].

**Non-stimulants act more narrowly.** Atomoxetine mainly blocks the noradrenaline transporter. Viloxazine also blocks it, more moderately, and acts on some serotonin receptors, so some researchers describe it as modulating both serotonin and noradrenaline; its mechanism in ADHD is not fully worked out [3].

Guanfacine and clonidine work differently. They stimulate a receptor called the alpha-2A adrenoceptor, which in the prefrontal cortex is thought to strengthen the signal that working-memory circuits hold on to [1].

**All the medicines studied beat placebo in short-term child trials.** A large 2018 network meta-analysis (a method that compares many treatments at once, here across 133 trials; it did not include viloxazine) looked at symptoms after about 12 weeks [4]. On clinicians' ratings, every medicine studied outperformed placebo in children, and amphetamines, methylphenidate, bupropion and atomoxetine did in adults, while modafinil did not [4]. On teachers' ratings, only methylphenidate and modafinil clearly did, and how large the effects are is covered on [How well does ADHD medication work?](how-well-medication-works.md)

**Which medicines are available differs by country.** Approval, licensed age groups and availability vary between countries and change over time. For example, viloxazine's extended-release form is approved for ADHD in the United States for children and adults [5]; no UK or EU licence for ADHD was found for this page. Modafinil's licensing status for ADHD was not confirmed for this page, and a prescriber or national medicines regulator can say what is approved where a reader lives.

**There is a "sweet spot", not a "more is better" curve.** Animal and human work on the prefrontal cortex shows an inverted-U relationship: moderate levels of dopamine and noradrenaline signalling improve focus and working memory, while levels that are too low or too high impair them [1]. This is one reason dose-finding with a prescriber matters and why more medicine does not always mean more benefit. Most of this mechanism comes from animal studies, so it is strong as an account of how the drugs act and weaker as an account of what causes ADHD.

**It is not only about dopamine.** Reviews of brain imaging and pharmacology describe both dopamine and noradrenaline modulating networks that run between the frontal lobes, the striatum (a deep structure involved in reward and action) and the cerebellum. The authors state plainly that the precise mechanisms of benefit "remain to be fully specified" [2]. Why "ADHD is low dopamine" is not an established fact is explained on [dopamine, noradrenaline and myths](../02-whats-happening-in-my-brain/dopamine-noradrenaline-and-myths.md).

**Stimulants affect people without ADHD too.** In a small placebo-controlled study published in 1980, a single dose of dextroamphetamine reduced activity and improved vigilance and learning in boys with ADHD, boys without ADHD and young men alike [6]. The men, but not the boys, reported euphoria [6]. So a good response to medication is not a diagnostic test, and a poor response does not rule ADHD out.

**Speed of action differs by drug class.** Stimulants work on the day they are taken and wear off over hours, depending on the formulation. Atomoxetine and the alpha-2A drugs usually take weeks rather than hours to reach their full effect.

A review of atomoxetine research in children, led by a manufacturer employee, reported that first responses can appear within a week but often build gradually and may not be robust until after about three months [7]. A checked guideline figure was not found for this page, and short trials of non-stimulants may underestimate them.

## What is uncertain or debated

**The link between the chemistry and the everyday effect is the weak point.** Researchers can show where the drugs bind and what they do to messenger levels. They cannot yet explain why one person's attention improves sharply while another's barely changes, or predict in advance who will respond to which drug.

**There is no test of "dopamine level" that could guide choice of medicine.** Brain imaging studies of the dopamine transporter in ADHD point in inconsistent directions, and some of that inconsistency may reflect earlier medication exposure [2]. Whether long-term stimulant use changes the brain's own transporter levels is debated and has not been settled.

**Most of the detail comes from animals and single doses.** Most of the detailed mechanism comes from rodents and monkeys, and from single doses given in laboratories. How it applies to years of daily use in adults is less well studied.

## What this means for you

Medicines for ADHD are not a crude sedative or a "speed" that forces focus. They adjust signalling in specific brain systems, and their effect depends on getting the amount right for the individual. Which medicine is used, and how much, is a decision for you and a prescriber; this page explains the mechanism and does not recommend any option.
