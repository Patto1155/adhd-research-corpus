# Real-life outcomes on ADHD medication: what do registries show?

> **Short answer:** During medicated periods, the same people had substantially fewer criminal convictions, crash-related emergency visits, substance-related emergencies and injuries. This within-person design, using Swedish national records and US insurance data, is much stronger than comparing different people. It is still observational, so it suggests rather than proves that medication caused the difference.

This page discusses suicide, death and accident statistics. The short answer above is the main point.

## Why the same-person design matters

**Comparing two groups is misleading.** People who take ADHD medication differ from people who do not in many ways: how severe their ADHD is, other conditions, family support, money, access to care. This is called confounding, and a specific version, confounding by indication, means the reason someone is prescribed a medicine is itself linked to the outcome. A simple comparison cannot tell whether a difference comes from the medicine or from who gets it.

**Each person becomes their own comparison.** A within-person design (also called a within-individual or self-controlled design) follows people over years and compares each person's rate of an outcome in months on medication with their own rate in months off it. Anything that stays fixed for that person, such as their genes, childhood, personality, sex and baseline severity, is automatically cancelled out, even if it was never measured [1][2].

**It is still not an experiment.** The design does not cancel things that *change over time* and also affect both medication and outcome. Someone might start medication after a crisis, stop during a chaotic period, or restart when life is calmer, and risk of some events is highest just before treatment starts, which can make medicated periods look better than they are [3].

Pharmacy records also show that a prescription was collected, not that it was taken. Only a randomised trial removes these problems, and trials of this length and size for outcomes like crime or crashes do not exist.

## What the evidence shows

**Criminal convictions were lower during medicated periods.** Among 25,656 Swedish people with ADHD followed from 2006 to 2009, the rate of criminal convictions was 32% lower in men and 41% lower in women during medicated periods than during their own unmedicated periods (hazard ratios 0.68 and 0.59) [1]. Across sensitivity analyses in men, the reduction ranged from 17% to 46%. The authors wrote that the findings "raise the possibility" that medication reduces criminality.

**Crash-related emergency visits were lower.** In US insurance records for 2.3 million adolescents and adults with ADHD, the odds of an emergency visit for a motor vehicle crash were 38% lower for men (odds ratio 0.62) and 42% lower for women (0.58) in months with medication than in the same people's months without [2]. Crashes that did not lead to medical care were not counted. Absolute crash rates per month were not extracted for this page.

**Substance-related emergencies were lower, and stayed lower later.** In US claims for about 3 million adolescents and adults, emergency visits linked to substance use were 35% lower in men (odds ratio 0.65) and 31% lower in women (0.69) in medicated months. Two years after a medicated period, the odds were still 19% lower in men and 14% lower in women (odds ratios 0.81 and 0.86) [4].

The authors concluded that medication was associated with lower risk at the time and, at least among men, lower later risk. Like the other studies here, this is an association, not evidence that ADHD medication treats addiction. The wider picture is on [Addiction and ADHD: is risk higher, and do medicines raise it?](../05-what-often-comes-with-it/alcohol-drugs-and-addiction.md)

**Injuries were less common with medication, at least short term.** A meta-analysis of 28 studies in children and adolescents found ADHD associated with about 1.5 times the odds of unintentional injury (odds ratio 1.53) [5]. Restricting to studies designed to avoid confounding by indication, mostly self-controlled, the authors concluded that medication has a protective effect, at least in the short term; a checked figure for the size of that effect was not found for this page [5].

A broader 2020 meta-analysis of 21 studies (mostly young people and stimulants) found medication associated with a protective effect on accidents and injuries, mood disorders and academic outcomes in its pooled analyses [6]. Its pooled estimates were seen only in a secondary summary, not in the abstract, so they are not quoted here. The abstract does not list criminality among the outcomes where the pooled analyses showed a benefit.

**Suicidal behaviour was not higher during treatment.** In 37,936 Swedish people with ADHD, the rate of suicide-related events was not higher during treatment; within-person it was slightly lower (hazard ratio 0.89 overall, range 0.79 to 1.00; 0.81 for stimulants) [7]. In another within-person study, months on medication were associated with lower odds of suicide attempts within the same people (odds ratio 0.61) [9].

**Suicide-attempt risk was already raised before treatment began.** A Hong Kong study of 25,629 young people prescribed methylphenidate found the rate of suicide attempts was already raised in the 90 days *before* treatment began, stayed raised in the first 90 days, and then returned to baseline; the authors did not find support for the medicine causing suicide attempts [3]. This is a warning against reading any rise around the start of treatment as caused by the medicine. Support options are listed on the [not medical advice](../08-reading-this-well/not-medical-advice.md) page.

**People taking medication had a lower death rate, but the evidence is thin.** A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of 19 observational studies reported that people with ADHD had a higher rate of death from any cause than people without ADHD (hazard ratio 1.71) [8]. Among people with ADHD, those taking prescribed medication had a lower rate of death from any cause [8]. The medication estimate rested on only three of the 19 studies, and the authors said these observational findings cannot show that medication itself reduces deaths.

The medication estimate itself was seen only in press coverage, so it is not quoted here. Registry findings on medication and mortality, including the absolute numbers needed to interpret them, are on [the hard statistics in context](../04-did-i-cause-this-and-where-is-it-going/the-hard-statistics-in-context.md).

## What is uncertain or debated

- **Time-varying confounding** is the main unresolved weakness, as set out above.
- **Where the data come from.** Most findings come from Sweden and US commercially insured people, so they may not transfer to other health systems.
- **Absolute rates are mostly missing.** Absolute rates were often not reported in the abstracts available for this page, so the relative reductions above cannot be turned into "how many events avoided per 1,000 people" here.
- **Funding was not reviewed.** Funding and conflict-of-interest statements were not reviewed individually for this page. Some research in this field is part-funded by drug manufacturers or has authors with industry ties, which does not make it wrong but which readers may reasonably check in each paper.

## What this means for you

These studies are among the strongest real-world evidence that ADHD medication is associated with fewer serious everyday harms, and they hold up in a design that removes many obvious explanations. They describe averages across very large groups and cannot promise any individual outcome. They say nothing about whether a particular person should take medication; that is a decision made with a prescriber.
